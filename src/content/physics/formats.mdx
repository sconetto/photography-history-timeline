## Where the names come from

**35mm and full frame.** Cinema film is 35 mm wide. When Oskar Barnack built the [[leica-i|Leica]], he laid the film sideways and doubled the cine frame to 24 × 36 mm. Kodak's [[format-135|135 cartridge]] made that frame the world standard. Digital sensors of the same size were called [[full-frame-digital|full frame]] because most early sensors were smaller.

**APS-C.** The [[aps-film|Advanced Photo System]] film of 1996 had a "classic" C print format of 25.1 × 16.7 mm. Early digital SLR sensors were about that size, so they were called [[aps-c|APS-C]], although the film itself was soon forgotten.

**Inch types.** Small sensors are named "1-inch" or "1/2.3-inch", sizes inherited from the glass tubes of 1950s video cameras. A "1-inch" sensor is only about 13.2 × 8.8 mm.

## Crop factor

A lens projects a round image; the sensor takes a rectangle from the middle. A smaller sensor takes a smaller piece, so the same lens shows a narrower view. The [[g:crop-factor|crop factor]] is the full-frame diagonal (43.27 mm) divided by the sensor's diagonal: about 1.5 for most APS-C sensors, 1.6 for Canon's, 2 for Four Thirds and 2.7 for 1-inch. A 50 mm lens on APS-C frames like a 75–80 mm lens on full frame.

## Equivalence

To get the *same* photograph on two formats (same framing, same depth of field, same total light) multiply both the focal length and the f-number by the crop factor. A 35 mm f/1.4 lens on APS-C behaves like a 52 mm f/2.1 lens on full frame. The exposure setting is still f/1.4: brightness per area is the same, but the smaller sensor collects less light in total, which is why it shows more noise.
