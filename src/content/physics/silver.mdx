## Silver salts darken

Silver chloride, bromide and iodide, the silver halides, darken in light. [[silver-salts|Schulze]] showed in the 1710s that light, not heat, caused it; [[scheele|Scheele]] found that violet light worked fastest and that the dark stuff was metallic silver. [[wedgwood-davy|Wedgwood and Davy]] made images this way around 1802 but could not stop them darkening. Printing-out papers such as the [[albumen-print]] still worked by direct darkening, which needs minutes to hours of sun.

## The latent image

A grain of silver bromide is a tiny crystal. When it absorbs a few photons, freed electrons gather at a defect and turn a few silver ions into a speck of metallic silver, a handful of atoms, far too small to see. This [[g:latent-image|latent image]] was the real discovery behind the [[daguerreotype]] and the [[calotype]], both of 1839–41: the picture is already there after a short exposure, only invisible.

## Development: chemical amplification

A [[g:developer|developer]] is a mild reducing agent. It cannot easily turn an untouched grain into silver, but a grain that carries a latent-image speck acts as a catalyst, and the whole grain, billions of atoms, becomes silver. One photon's worth of change can be multiplied up to many millions of times. This is why exposures fell from hours to minutes once development was used, and to seconds with the [[wet-collodion]] process. The [[gelatin-dry-plate]] of the 1870s, with grains ripened in warm gelatin, made exposures short enough to hold the camera in the hand.

## Fixing

The grains that saw no light are still sensitive, so the image would fog as soon as it met daylight. A fixer dissolves them. [[hypo-fixer|Herschel]] found in 1819 that sodium thiosulfate ("hypo") dissolves silver halides, and suggested it for photography in 1839; it is still used. Washing removes the fixer, which would otherwise stain and fade the image.

## Grain and speed

A bigger crystal catches more light, so emulsions with large grains are faster but look [[g:grain|grainier]]. Every film is a compromise between speed and fine detail. Silver halides are naturally sensitive only to blue and violet light; dyes added from the 1870s ([[colour-sensitising]]) extended them to green ([[g:orthochromatic|orthochromatic]]) and then red ([[g:panchromatic|panchromatic]]). Colour films stack three such layers, each turned into a coloured dye during development ([[agfacolor-neu]], [[kodachrome]]).
