## A ratio, not a size

The [[g:aperture|aperture]] is the opening light passes through. What matters for brightness is not its size alone but its size compared with the focal length, because a longer lens spreads the same light over a bigger image. The [[g:f-number|f-number]] is that ratio: N = f / D. A 50 mm lens with a 25 mm opening is at f/2; a 200 mm lens needs a 100 mm opening for the same f/2. Every lens at the same f-number puts the same brightness on the film, which is what lets a light meter give one reading for any lens.

## Stops

Light through a circle goes with its area, so with the square of the diameter. Halving the light means dividing the area by two and the diameter by √2 ≈ 1.41. That gives the familiar sequence f/1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16, 22: each step is one [[g:stop|stop]] and halves the light. Cameras also offer half and third stops in between. The [[petzval-lens]] of 1840 worked at about f/3.6, against roughly f/14 for the lens on the first daguerreotype cameras, which cut portrait exposures from minutes to under a minute.

## Depth of field

A lens focuses exactly at one distance. Points nearer or further land as small blur discs, and if a disc is smaller than the eye can resolve in the final print (by convention 0.03 mm on 35mm film), the point looks sharp. A smaller opening narrows the cone of light, so the discs shrink and the [[g:depth-of-field|depth of field]] grows. Focus at the hyperfocal distance and everything from half that distance to infinity looks sharp: the basis of fixed-focus snapshots from the [[kodak-camera]] and [[brownie]] to the [[instamatic]].

## The diffraction limit

Stopping down has a price. Light passing an edge spreads out ([[g:diffraction|diffraction]]), so a point never images as a point but as a small pattern, the Airy disk, about 2.44 λN across. At f/2.8 that is under 4 µm, smaller than a typical [[g:pixel|pixel]]; at f/22 it is about 30 µm, several pixels wide, and fine detail softens. For most lenses the sharpest setting sits two or three stops down from wide open, where aberrations have faded but diffraction has not yet taken over. A [[p:pinhole]] lives entirely at the diffraction end.
