## Beginnings

Polaroid was built on one man's inventions. Edwin Land was born in 1909 in Bridgeport, Connecticut, and went to Harvard to study chemistry and physics, but left without a degree to work on an idea: a cheap sheet that could polarize light, letting through only light waves vibrating in one direction. Such a filter could cut the glare of car headlights and reflections. In 1928, at 19, he made the first synthetic sheet polarizer, and he patented it in 1929.

In 1932 Land set up Land-Wheelwright Laboratories with his Harvard physics instructor to turn the invention into products. One of the first customers for its polarizing filters was Eastman Kodak. The company was incorporated as the Polaroid Corporation in 1937, named after its product. Polaroid sheet went into sunglasses, scientific instruments, windows and 3D movie glasses, and in the Second World War Polaroid made goggles, target finders and filters for rangefinders and periscopes for the armed forces.

## Growth and turning points

During the war, on a family holiday in Santa Fe, New Mexico, Land's three-year-old daughter asked why she could not see the picture he had just taken of her. Land spent the next years working out how a camera could develop its own pictures. On 21 February 1947 he showed the answer to the Optical Society of America in New York: a pod of chemicals, burst and spread between a negative and a sheet of paper as the film was pulled out of the camera, made a finished print in about a minute.

The first [[instant-photography|Polaroid Land Camera]], the Model 95, went on sale at the Jordan Marsh department store in Boston in November 1948, just before Christmas. It cost nearly $90, but the first cameras sold out on the first day. Instant photography became a huge business: in 1977 alone Polaroid sold more than six million cameras. Polaroid improved the film again and again: faster, sharper, and in 1959 a black-and-white film rated at ISO 3000. In 1963 it brought out Polacolor, the first instant colour film, in a peel-apart pack, and from 1965 the Swinger, at under $20, brought instant pictures to a much wider market.

## What it made

Land's goal was a camera with nothing to peel, time or throw away. The [[sx-70|SX-70]] took about twelve years and, by some estimates, as much as a billion dollars to develop. Land showed it to Polaroid's board in April 1972 by pulling it from his jacket pocket, unfolding it and firing off five pictures in quick succession, and it went on sale that autumn. The camera was a folding single-lens reflex; a motor pushed each picture out, and the print developed in daylight before the user's eyes. *Life* magazine put Land and the camera on its cover.

Polaroid film was used far beyond the family album. Professionals used Polaroid backs on studio and medium-format cameras to check lighting and exposure before shooting on film, and the company made cameras for passports, ID cards, science and medicine. From the late 1960s it gave film and studio space to artists such as Ansel Adams, Andy Warhol and William Wegman, and built up a large collection of their pictures.

When [[m:kodak|Kodak]] brought out its own instant cameras in 1976, Polaroid sued for patent infringement within days. It won: in 1986 the courts ordered Kodak out of instant photography, and in 1991 Kodak paid Polaroid $925 million.

## Legacy

Land's last big project, Polavision, an instant home-movie system of 1977, failed in the shops. He stepped down as chairman in 1980, founded the Rowland Institute for Science at Harvard, and died in 1991. Without him, Polaroid struggled. One-hour photo labs, single-use cameras, camcorders and finally digital cameras ate into its market, and it came late to digital photography.

On 11 October 2001 Polaroid filed for bankruptcy. Its brand and business were sold, and in 2005 they went to Petters Group Worldwide, which itself collapsed in a fraud case in 2008, and Polaroid went into bankruptcy a second time. That same year Polaroid announced it would stop making instant film and closed its film factories. A group led by Florian Kaps and former Polaroid staff bought the factory in Enschede, in the Netherlands, and as the [[m:impossible|Impossible Project]] set out to make instant film again. In 2017 Impossible's main shareholder bought the Polaroid brand, and the company has traded as Polaroid since.
