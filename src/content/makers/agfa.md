## Beginnings

Agfa began with dyes, not photographs. In 1867 the chemists Paul Mendelssohn Bartholdy and Carl Alexander von Martius set up a factory in Rummelsburg, near Berlin, to make aniline dyes, the bright synthetic colours made from coal tar. In 1873 it became a public company, the Actien-Gesellschaft für Anilin-Fabrikation, and its initials gave it the name Agfa.

The chemist Momme Andresen led it into photography. From 1888 Agfa made photographic chemicals, and in 1891 it brought out Rodinal, a black-and-white developer that stayed a standard for more than a century. Agfa registered its name as a trademark in 1897, and its plates and films for the new X-rays helped make its name for quality.

## Growth and turning points

Film came next. In 1908 Agfa launched a cine film that burned less readily than the dangerous nitrate film of the time, and in 1909 it founded a big film factory at Wolfen, between Dessau and Halle. In 1925 Agfa was one of the German chemical companies, with Bayer, BASF and Hoechst, that merged into IG Farben. Through the merger it took over a camera works in Munich, and in 1926 it brought out its first real Agfa camera. In 1928 it bought the American company Ansco and launched the Billy folding camera.

Agfa made photography cheap in Germany. Its first box camera for roll film came in 1930, and it sold one model, the Box 44, for just four Reichsmarks, at a loss, earning its money back on the film. In 1936 the Wolfen factory produced [[agfacolor-neu|Agfacolor Neu]], a colour film whose dye couplers sat in the film itself, so it could be developed in one process. Most later colour films followed the same idea. In 1942 Agfa added colour negative film and colour paper for still photography.

IG Farben was deeply involved with the Nazi regime. It used slave labour during the war and took part in developing Zyklon B, the gas used in the death camps. After the war the Allies broke it up.

## What it made

The war split Agfa, like Zeiss. Wolfen was in the Soviet zone, so in the West film production restarted at Bayer's site in Leverkusen in 1945, and Agfa became a Bayer subsidiary. Wolfen kept making film under the Agfa name until a long dispute with West Germany ended in 1964: from then on its products were sold as ORWO, for "Original Wolfen".

In the West, Agfa became a big maker of cameras for ordinary people. The Silette of 1954 was an affordable 35 mm camera, the Isolette folding cameras were popular for medium format, and the Automatic 66 of 1956 already set its own exposure. The Optima of 1959 went further: it was one of a handful of cameras that year that set both shutter speed and aperture by themselves, so the user only had to aim and press. From 1968 many Agfa cameras had a red "sensor" shutter button, and in the 1970s its Agfamatic pocket cameras for 110 film sold widely in Germany.

In 1964 Agfa merged with Gevaert, a Belgian maker of photographic paper and film. The photographer Lieven Gevaert had begun making photographic paper in Antwerp in 1890. Agfa-Gevaert was Europe's largest photographic company, half owned by Bayer.

## Legacy

Japanese competition hit Agfa's cameras hard. Its last own cameras came in the early 1980s, partly made by Chinon in Japan, and it stopped making cameras in 1983. Agfa-Gevaert moved into digital imaging and medical and printing systems, and in 1999 Bayer floated it on the stock market, with its headquarters in Mortsel, Belgium.

Film declined fast with digital photography. On 2 November 2004 Agfa-Gevaert sold its consumer photo business to a management buyout, which ran it as AgfaPhoto. In May 2005 AgfaPhoto filed for insolvency, and parts of the Leverkusen factories and machinery were sold. The AgfaPhoto name lives on, licensed to other companies for films and cameras, while Agfa-Gevaert still makes imaging systems for hospitals and printers. In Wolfen, small companies have made products under the ORWO name again since the late 1990s.
