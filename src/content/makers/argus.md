## Beginnings

Argus began as a radio company. In 1931, in the depths of the Depression, a group of local businessmen in Ann Arbor, Michigan, set up the International Radio Corporation to bring jobs to the town. Among them were William E. Brown Jr., later the town's mayor, and Charles Verschoor, who became the company's president. Its radios, sold as Kadettes, had cabinets moulded from plastic rather than made of wood, so they could be sold cheaply, and the company took on 75 workers in its first year.

But radios were a seasonal business: they sold well in autumn and winter and badly in spring and summer. Verschoor looked for something to keep the factory busy all year, and he chose a camera. Colour slides on [[kodachrome|Kodachrome]] were catching on, but the German 35mm cameras from [[m:leica|Leica]] and [[m:zeiss|Zeiss]] cost more than most Americans could pay. Verschoor's idea was to make a 35mm camera the way he made radios, with a moulded plastic body.

## Growth and turning points

The Argus A went on sale in May 1936 for $12.50, an unheard-of price for a [[format-135|35mm camera]]. Some 30,000 were sold in the first week, and by the time production stopped in the war about half a million A-series cameras had been made. The camera was such a success that the company sold its radio patents, renamed itself the International Research Corporation and made cameras its main business. Verschoor did not last: unhappy shareholders pushed him out in 1938.

That year Argus brought out the Argus C and C2, boxy cameras with rangefinders designed by Gustave Fassin, a freelance designer from Belgium. In 1939 came the [[argus-c3|Argus C3]], a C2 with flash sockets on the side. It was heavy, rectangular and hard to hold, and people called it the Brick, but it was cheap, tough and simple enough to repair with ordinary tools. It stayed in production, barely changed, until 1966.

From 1940 Argus made optical equipment for the American armed forces, and from August 1942 it made nothing else, turning out military optics and radios until the end of the war. It won the Army-Navy "E" Award for war production five times, and in 1944 it renamed itself Argus, Inc.

## What it made

After the war Argus went back to cameras for amateurs, the middle of the market that few other American makers were serving. It called itself Argus Cameras, Inc. from 1949, and through the 1950s it was the second-largest camera company in the United States, after [[m:kodak|Kodak]]. Besides the C3 it sold the Argoflex twin-lens reflexes, the cheap Seventy-Five box camera with its big reflex finder, the sleeker C4 rangefinder of 1951 and its interchangeable-lens version, the C44. Its slide projectors became one of its most important products, and in the late 1950s a new factory in South Carolina was opened to keep up with demand.

The C3 outlasted every other camera Argus made. More than two million were built, and for a generation of Americans it was the camera they learned 35mm on, often loaded with colour slide film.

## Legacy

In 1957 the electronics company Sylvania bought Argus, hoping its flashbulbs would sell alongside Argus cameras. But Japanese cameras were arriving that offered more for less, and Argus's later models, with built-in meters and more complicated mechanisms, never matched the C3's success. In 1962 Sylvania sold Argus to Mansfield, a Chicago importer of photographic products. Camera making in Ann Arbor had stopped by 1963, most Argus products of the 1960s were made abroad, several by [[m:mamiya|Mamiya]], with the Argus name on them, and by 1969 all American production had ended.

The Argus name has since passed through several owners, which sold imported cameras under it. The old factory in Ann Arbor is now offices, and its upstairs lobby holds the Argus Museum. The C3 is still easy to find, and because it is so simple, it is still easy to repair and use.
