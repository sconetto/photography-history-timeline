## Beginnings

Takeshi Yamashita founded Takachiho Seisakusho in Tokyo on 12 October 1919. Japan imported its microscopes, and he wanted to make them at home. In 1920 the company launched its first, the Asahi microscope. It also made thermometers. In February 1921 it introduced a new brand for its products, Olympus, after the mountain home of the Greek gods. The "OLYMPUS TOKYO" mark is still in use.

The company turned to photography in the 1930s. It developed its own camera lenses, called Zuiko, and in 1936 launched its first camera, the Semi-Olympus I, with a Zuiko lens. In 1942 the firm became Takachiho Optical, and in 1949 Olympus Optical Co., Ltd.

## Growth and turning points

In 1950 Olympus made the first practical gastrocamera, a tiny camera on a flexible tube that photographed the inside of the stomach. It grew into the company's endoscope business, which in time became its main one.

In cameras, Olympus's strength was small size. In 1959 it asked a young engineer, Yoshihisa Maitani, to design a camera that would sell for ¥6,000. His [[olympus-pen|Olympus Pen]] used the half frame, 18 × 24 mm, so a roll of 36 gave 72 pictures. It started a half-frame boom in Japan. The Pen F of 1963 turned the idea into an [[g:slr|SLR]] system, still the only half-frame one ever made.

Maitani then worked on a full-frame SLR much smaller than the others of its day. It was launched in 1972 as the M-1. Leica, which used M names for its own cameras, asked Olympus to change it, and from 1973 it was the OM-1. It was the world's smallest and lightest 35mm SLR, with a quiet shutter and a cushioned mirror, and it began the OM system. The OM-2 of 1975 metered light off the film surface during the exposure, which also let it control flash automatically.

Olympus did just as well with compact cameras. The Trip 35 of 1968 sold for about 20 years. The XA of 1979 was a pocket camera with a sliding lens cover, and the AF-1 of 1986 was, Olympus says, the first fully automatic waterproof compact. The μ (mju) series of 1991 became its flagship compact line.

## What it made

Through most of its history Olympus was a company of microscopes and medical optics as much as cameras. Its microscopes went from the Asahi of 1920 to research instruments; its gastrocamera led to fibre-optic and then video endoscopes, and Olympus became a leading maker of endoscopes. It also made audio recorders, including the first microcassette recorder, in 1969.

In digital photography, Olympus was early. Its first digital camera for the general public, the CAMEDIA C-800L, came in 1996. It then bet on a smaller sensor. With Kodak it proposed the [[four-thirds|Four Thirds]] standard, and the E-1 of 2003 was the first camera built to it. The E-330 of 2006 was, Olympus says, the first SLR with live view on its rear screen. In 2008 Olympus and [[m:panasonic|Panasonic]] announced Micro Four Thirds, a [[g:mirrorless|mirrorless]] version of the system. Olympus's first, the Pen E-P1 of 2009, revived the Pen name, and the OM-D E-M5 of 2012 revived the OM idea with what Olympus calls the first five-axis sensor-shift [[image-stabilisation|stabiliser]].

## Today

The company changed its name to Olympus Corporation in 2003. In 2011 it admitted that it had hidden losses for years, a scandal that shook it badly. Meanwhile, as phones took over everyday photography, camera sales fell across the industry. In 2020 Olympus agreed to sell its camera business to Japan Industrial Partners, an investment firm. The new company, [[m:om-digital|OM Digital Solutions]], took it over on 1 January 2021 and now sells the cameras under the OM System name.

In 2023 Olympus also transferred its microscope and scientific business, which now trades as Evident. What remains is a medical company, built on the gastrocamera of 1950. It still uses the Olympus name, while the cameras that made it famous are made by someone else.
