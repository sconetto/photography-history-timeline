## Beginnings

Phase One started in Copenhagen in the early 1990s, as the first digital cameras were reaching professional studios. A 2003 press release called it a ten-year-old firm; the company now gives its founding year as 1994. Its first product was not a camera but a back: the StudioKit, sold from 1994, fitted to medium-format and large-format studio cameras in place of film.

The StudioKit was a scanning back. Like a flatbed scanner, it moved a single line of sensors across the image and built the picture up row by row. A scan took minutes, so it only worked for still subjects such as products, paintings and studio still lifes, and it needed a computer beside the camera. But the files were far larger and cleaner than any early digital camera could make, which suited advertising and catalogue photographers who had to print big.

## Growth and turning points

In 1998 Phase One launched LightPhase, a one-shot back of 6 megapixels for Hasselblad bodies. It took the whole picture at once with an area sensor, so people and moving subjects could be photographed digitally with a medium-format camera. Over the next decade Phase One's backs grew to 16, 22, 39 and then, with the P65+ of 2008, 60 megapixels, and they could be fitted to cameras from several makers.

The software that ran the backs became a business of its own. In 2002 Phase One brought its raw converter, Capture One, to Canon's new digital SLRs, then to other brands. By 2003 sales were climbing fast and Phase One set up a separate product group for it. Capture One became known for its colour and for tethered shooting, with the camera wired to a computer, which studio photographers relied on.

In 2009 Phase One became a major shareholder in Mamiya Digital Imaging, the Japanese company behind the [[m:mamiya|Mamiya]] medium-format cameras that many of its backs were used on, and it also took a stake in Leaf, a rival maker of digital backs. The two brands were later combined as Mamiya Leaf. The 645 camera it had already designed with Mamiya was sold under both names, and in 2015 Phase One bought Mamiya's digital business outright.

## What it made

Phase One's cameras stayed at the very top of the market, in price as well as resolution. In 2015 it launched the XF, a medium-format SLR body of its own design with its own autofocus and a touch screen, paired with IQ3 backs of up to 80 megapixels. In 2018 the IQ4 150MP back brought a 151-megapixel back-side illuminated sensor, a first for medium format, and ran Capture One's image processing inside the back.

Alongside studio and landscape photographers, Phase One found other customers who needed the sharpest possible images: aerial mapping cameras flown in aircraft, industrial inspection, and the digitising of paintings, books and manuscripts for museums and archives.

## Today

The Danish investment firm Axcel bought Phase One in 2019 and split it in two: Phase One kept the cameras, and the software became a separate company, Capture One. Phase One is still based in Copenhagen. It now describes itself first as an aerial imaging company, and also makes systems for cultural heritage digitising and for photographers. Its cameras are built to be upgraded part by part rather than replaced, and it offers repairs on products up to twenty years old.
