## Beginnings

Nippon Kōgaku K.K., "Japan Optical", was founded in Tokyo on 25 July 1917. Japan wanted its own makers of precision optics, and Koyata Iwasaki, head of the Mitsubishi group, backed a new company that brought several small optical firms together. It started with rangefinders, microscopes and binoculars, and in 1918 it began research into optical glass, which Japan then had to import.

In 1921 eight German engineers came to work with the company, among them the lens designers Max Lange and Hermann Dillmann. Their teaching shaped a generation of Japanese optical designers. Nippon Kōgaku delivered its first camera lenses, for aerial photography, in 1933, under a name it had adopted the year before: Nikkor.

Before the company made a camera of its own, its lenses were already on one. The Hansa Canon of 1936 used a Nikkor lens, mount, viewfinder and rangefinder, all made by Nippon Kōgaku, for the young company that became [[m:canon|Canon]].

## Growth and turning points

During the Second World War Nippon Kōgaku made optics for the Imperial Japanese Navy: rangefinders, periscopes and binoculars. Peace left it with skilled staff and nothing to make. It turned to microscopes, surveying instruments and a small 35mm camera. Staff first thought of calling it "Nikorette", then chose a stronger name built on "Nikko", the company's short name: Nikon.

The Nikon I went on sale in 1948, and it might have stayed a footnote. The change came from the Korean War. LIFE photographers in Tokyo, among them David Douglas Duncan, tried Nikkor lenses on their Leicas and were impressed. In the Korean winter, they reported, Nikon cameras kept working when others froze. On 10 December 1950 *The New York Times* wrote about Nikon's cameras and lenses, and photographers outside Japan began to take them seriously.

The rangefinder line peaked with the SP of 1957. Two years later Nikon put its parts into a new body with a mirror, a [[g:pentaprism|pentaprism]] and a bayonet mount. The [[nikon-f|Nikon F]] of 1959 was not the first Japanese [[g:slr|SLR]], but it was the first complete professional system: interchangeable finders, a motor drive, dozens of lenses. Within a few years it had replaced the rangefinder on news assignments, including in Vietnam. The F was made until 1973, and the F mount it introduced was used for 59 years.

Nikon then kept a steady professional line, each model compatible with the lenses before it: the F2 (1971), the F3 (1980), the F4 (1988), the F5 (1996) and the F6 (2004). Nikons went to space with NASA, from a modified Photomic FTN on Apollo 15 in 1971 to the F3s on the Space Shuttle. The company was slower to [[autofocus|autofocus]]: its first SLR that focused from the body, the F-501, came in 1986. On 1 April 1988 Nippon Kōgaku became Nikon Corporation.

## What it made

Nikon's cameras are only part of its story. Its microscopes, surveying instruments and, from 1980, the "steppers" used to print circuits on silicon wafers made it as much an industrial optics company as a camera maker.

In cameras, its strongest years were the turns from one technology to the next:

- **Rangefinder to SLR**, with the F.
- **Film to digital**, with the [[nikon-d1|D1]] of 1999. Earlier professional digital cameras, such as [[kodak-dcs|Kodak's DCS series]], were film bodies with digital backs. A team of about ten people designed the D1 from scratch. It had a 2.7-[[g:megapixel|megapixel]] [[aps-c|APS-C]]-sized sensor and cost far less than earlier professional digital SLRs, and newspapers bought it in large numbers.
- **Underwater**, with the Nikonos (1963). It was developed from the Calypso, a French camera designed for Jacques Cousteau's divers.

The D3 of 2007 was Nikon's first [[full-frame-digital|full-frame]] digital SLR, and its clean images at high [[g:iso|ISO]] changed what sports and news photographers could shoot by available light.

## Today

Nikon moved to [[g:mirrorless|mirrorless]] full frame in 2018 with the Z 7 and Z 6. Their Z mount is far wider than the F mount (55 mm across), which gives lens designers more freedom. The Z 9 of 2021, Nikon's first professional mirrorless flagship, has no mechanical shutter at all: its stacked sensor reads out fast enough to do without one. In 2024 thirteen of them went to the International Space Station. In 2024 Nikon bought RED, the American maker of digital cinema cameras, and the two companies now share their technology.

Nikon remains independent and still makes cameras, Nikkor lenses, microscopes, measuring instruments and chip-making lithography systems. By 2024 it had made 120 million Nikkor lenses for interchangeable-lens cameras.
