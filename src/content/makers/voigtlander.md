## Beginnings

Voigtländer is one of the oldest names in optics. In 1756 Johann Christoph Voigtländer, a carpenter's son from Leipzig, opened a workshop in Vienna to make precision instruments such as compasses and sundials, and devices for measuring distances and angles. A royal licence from the Empress Maria Theresa let him work largely without competition. He died in 1797. In 1808 his son Johann Friedrich set up his own optical business, making opera glasses and lenses for spectacles, after touring the best optical workshops in Germany and England.

The third generation took the firm into photography. Peter Wilhelm Friedrich Voigtländer took over in 1837, and from 1839 the business was called Voigtländer & Sohn. Photography had just been announced, and portraits were what people wanted, but the daguerreotype needed minutes of exposure in bright light.

## Growth and turning points

In 1840 the Hungarian mathematician Josef Petzval calculated a new portrait lens for Voigtländer. The [[petzval-lens|Petzval lens]], working at about f/3.6, let in far more light than the lenses then in use and cut exposures to a minute or two. To go with it, Voigtländer built a camera made entirely of metal, a brass cone on a stand, which went into series production in 1841. By 1842 about 600 had been sold, and Petzval-type portrait lenses were made for roughly a century.

Success needed a bigger factory. In 1849 Voigtländer opened a second works in Braunschweig, in northern Germany, chosen for its good railway links, and in 1862 the company moved its headquarters there. The Vienna works closed later. In 1898 the family firm became a public company, and new lens designs followed: the Heliar of 1900, the Dynar and the Collinear, and later the Skopar and Ultron that went on Voigtländer's own cameras. Voigtländer lenses were also sold in New York for use on Kodak cameras.

In 1920 two Voigtländer men, Paul Franke and Reinhold Heidecke, left to found their own company in Braunschweig. Heidecke had twice offered Voigtländer his design for a twin-lens reflex camera, and twice it was turned down. As Franke & Heidecke, later [[m:rollei|Rollei]], they made it the Rolleiflex. In 1925 the chemicals company Schering bought a majority of Voigtländer's shares.

## What it made

Between the wars Voigtländer became one of the world's big camera makers. The Bessa of 1929, a 6 × 9 cm folding camera for roll film, sold for almost thirty years, and a later version, the Bessa RF of 1935, had a coupled rangefinder. The Brillant of 1931 looked like a twin-lens reflex but was at first a simple box camera; it only focused like one from 1938. The Vito of 1939 was its first compact 35 mm camera.

After the war came the Vitessa, a metal 35 mm rangefinder camera with a folding lens, and the rigid-lens Vito B. In 1958 Voigtländer launched the Bessamatic, a 35 mm [[g:slr|SLR]] with a leaf shutter behind the lens, and the Ultramatic of 1961 added automatic exposure. The Ultramatic CS of 1965 was among the first SLRs to set the exposure automatically from a meter reading through the lens. In 1959 it sold the Voigtländer-Zoomar 36–82 mm, the first [[zoom-lens|zoom lens]] for 35 mm still cameras. The Vitrona of 1964 was, according to Digital Camera World, the first 35 mm camera with a built-in electronic flash.

## Legacy

In 1956 Schering sold its Voigtländer shares to [[m:zeiss|Carl Zeiss]], and Voigtländer was run together with Zeiss Ikon. The two old names could not hold off the Japanese makers. Sales fell, and the Braunschweig camera factory closed in August 1971. The works passed to a joint company of Zeiss, Rollei and the state of Lower Saxony, which made mostly lenses for Zeiss Ikon and Rollei cameras.

In 1974 Rollei, Voigtländer's old offshoot, took full control. The Voigtländer name went on Rollei's own SLRs and compact cameras, many made in Rollei's factory in Singapore, until Rollei's financial trouble in the early 1980s. In 1997 the name was bought by Ringfoto, a German photo retail group, and licensed to the Japanese maker Cosina. From 1999 Cosina made Voigtländer Bessa rangefinder cameras for Leica-mount lenses, and it still makes Voigtländer lenses today, including fast manual-focus lenses for Leica M and most mirrorless mounts.
