## Beginnings

Canon began with a camera nobody could buy. In November 1933 Goro Yoshida, a repairer of film cameras, set up the Precision Optical Instruments Laboratory in a Tokyo apartment with his brother-in-law Saburo Uchida and Takeo Maeda. Yoshida had taken a Leica II apart to see how it worked, and he wanted Japan to have a 35mm [[g:rangefinder|rangefinder]] of its own. He called his prototype the Kwanon, after the Buddhist goddess of mercy. It was advertised but never sold, and Yoshida left the laboratory in 1934.

The team still had no lenses or rangefinders of its own. They turned to Nippon Kōgaku, the optical company that later became [[m:nikon|Nikon]]. In the Hansa Canon of 1936, Nippon Kōgaku made the Nikkor lens, the mount, the viewfinder optics and the rangefinder; the laboratory made the body and the [[g:focal-plane-shutter|focal-plane shutter]]. "Hansa" was the trademark of the Omiya camera shop, which sold it. The name "Kwanon" became "Canon", a word for a standard to judge by.

In 1937 the laboratory was incorporated as Precision Optical Industry, Co. Ltd., and production began in earnest.

## Growth and turning points

Takeshi Mitarai, an obstetrician and a friend of Uchida, became president in 1942. After the war he set the company a blunt goal: catch up with the Leica, then surpass it. Canon sold cameras to Allied soldiers in occupied Japan, and those customers found the names confusing: Canon for cameras, Serenar for lenses, Precision Optical Industry for the maker. In 1947 the company became Canon Camera Co., Inc., and its own lenses replaced the Nikkors.

The 1950s brought a stream of rangefinders, including the first Japanese camera with a 1/1000 s shutter (the III, 1951). The Leica M3 of 1954 showed that rangefinders had a strong rival on the way. Canon moved to the [[g:slr|single-lens reflex]] with the Canonflex in May 1959, a month before the [[nikon-f|Nikon F]]. The Nikon F won that round. Canon's answer was patience: the F-1 of 1971 was a professional system from its first day, with FD lenses that metered at full aperture.

The great turn came in 1976. Since early 1974 about a hundred engineers had worked on an automatic SLR that anyone could afford. The [[canon-ae1|AE-1]] used a microprocessor to set the [[g:exposure|exposure]] and far fewer parts than earlier cameras, and it was built on automated lines. Canon advertised it on national television with famous tennis and golf players, a first for a Japanese camera maker, and it became a runaway hit. The A-1 (1978) went further, with five exposure modes.

Canon was slow to [[autofocus|autofocus]]. Minolta's Maxxum 7000 took the market in 1985. Canon took two years and then changed everything. The EOS 650 of March 1987 used a new, fully electronic [[g:lens-mount|lens mount]], EF, with a motor in every lens and no mechanical links to the body. Owners of FD lenses were angry. But the design let Canon put ultrasonic focusing motors (USM) and, from 1995, optical [[image-stabilisation|image stabilisation]] into its lenses, and the EOS-1 of 1989 won over professional photographers.

The company had already grown far beyond cameras. It made its first calculator in 1964 and its first plain-paper copier in 1970. In 1969 it dropped "Camera" from its name and became Canon Inc.

## What it made

Canon's cameras run from the Hansa Canon to today's mirrorless bodies, but three lines shaped photography most.

- **The AE-1 and its family**, which made automatic SLRs ordinary.
- **EOS**, the system that has lasted from 1987 to now. It took Canon from film SLRs to digital SLRs without a change of mount.
- **Its own sensors.** The EOS D30 (2000) used a [[cmos-sensor|CMOS sensor]] that Canon designed and made itself, at a time when most digital cameras used CCDs. Making sensors in-house let Canon offer large ones cheaply: the EOS 5D of 2005 brought [[full-frame-digital|full-frame digital]] to many more photographers.

The [[g:full-frame|full-frame]] EOS 5D Mark II of 2008 recorded Full HD video, the first digital SLR to do so. Its big sensor gave video a shallow, cinematic look, and it was soon used on television series and films. Canon followed it with the Cinema EOS line in 2011. Along the way Canon also made compact Canonets and PowerShots, 8mm cine cameras, broadcast lenses and, since 1987, a large share of the world's camera lenses.

## Today

In 2018 Canon introduced the EOS R system: [[g:mirrorless|mirrorless]] full-frame cameras on a new RF mount, with adapters for EF lenses. It now makes EOS R bodies from entry-level to professional, cinema cameras and broadcast lenses. Cameras are only part of a much larger company that also makes printers, office machines, medical imaging and the lithography equipment used to make chips. Canon still makes its own sensors, and for many years it has sold more interchangeable-lens cameras than any other maker.
