## Beginnings

In February 2008 [[m:polaroid|Polaroid]] announced that it would stop making instant film and close its last film factories, in Mexico and the Netherlands. Digital cameras had taken its market, and it had already stopped making cameras. Millions of Polaroid cameras around the world were about to become useless.

Florian Kaps, who had found a Polaroid camera at a flea market in 2003 and opened an online shop for Polaroid film in 2005, did not accept that. A few months after the announcement he went to a closing event at the factory in Enschede, in the Netherlands, and met André Bosman, who worked there. With Marwan Saba and a team of former Polaroid staff, they set out to raise private money to save the machines. Kaps took his motto from Edwin Land himself: do not undertake a project unless it is manifestly important and nearly impossible. The new company was called the Impossible Project.

## Growth and turning points

In late 2008 Impossible bought Polaroid's production machinery for over $3 million, as the factory was about to be scrapped, and leased part of the old Polaroid plant in Enschede. But it had the machines without the recipes. Polaroid's patents and other intellectual property had not been part of the deal. Instant film is one of the most chemically complex things people make, and Impossible had to reinvent it from scratch.

On 22 March 2010 it released its first two films, the black-and-white PX 100 and PX 600 Silver Shade, made with help from [[m:harman|Harman]], the British maker of Ilford film. Colour film followed that summer. The early films were unpredictable: users were told to keep packs in the fridge, airport X-ray machines could spoil them, and in less than perfect conditions the results were mixed. Fans put up with it, and as more film was made, the quality improved. Impossible also refurbished and sold old Polaroid cameras, so new photographers could buy a camera and film from the same place.

## What it made

In 2012 the Smolokowski family bought a 20% stake in the company. Kaps left in 2013, and in 2014 Oskar Smolokowski, then in his mid-twenties, became chief executive. In 2016 Impossible brought out its first camera of its own design, the I-1, with a ring of LED lights around the lens and an iPhone app to control it.

In May 2017 Impossible's largest shareholder bought the Polaroid brand and its intellectual property. Four months later the Impossible Project disappeared, replaced by Polaroid Originals. It celebrated with the OneStep 2, forty years after Polaroid's original OneStep, and an updated I-Type film, which was far more reliable than Impossible's early stock. The company the old Polaroid had left behind now owned its name.

## Today

On 26 March 2020 Polaroid Originals became simply Polaroid, with one company, one website and one brand. It launched the Polaroid Now camera and new black-frame and colour-frame films at the same time. The company still makes its film in Enschede, in the factory that Kaps and Bosman saved, and sells new cameras alongside film for old SX-70 and 600 cameras.

Impossible arrived at the start of the wider [[film-revival|film revival]], and it helped prove that film still had a market. It was not alone: [[m:fujifilm|Fujifilm]]'s Instax cameras, which had sold 100,000 in 2004, sold five million in 2015. But Impossible did something rare: it brought a photographic process that had been declared dead back to life, almost from nothing.
