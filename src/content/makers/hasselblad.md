## Beginnings

The Hasselblad name was famous in Sweden long before the cameras. Fritz Victor Hasselblad founded a trading company in Gothenburg in 1841. In 1885 his son Arvid met George Eastman while on honeymoon in London, and the family firm became [[m:kodak|Kodak]]'s agent in Sweden and ran a chain of photo shops. Victor Hasselblad, born into the family in 1906, learned the trade abroad, including a spell with Eastman in Rochester, and in 1937 opened his own photo shop, Victor Foto, in Gothenburg. He was also a keen bird photographer.

In 1940, during the Second World War, the Swedish air force asked him to copy a German aerial camera that had been found intact in Sweden. He answered that he would build a better one. He set up a workshop in a shed in central Gothenburg, worked out how the German camera was made, and in 1941 his small company, Ross AB, began making the hand-held HK-7. About 240 were made, and with later aerial cameras such as the SKa4, which had interchangeable film magazines, Hasselblad delivered 342 cameras to the military by 1945. When his father died in 1942, Victor took over the family business and the camera firm became Victor Hasselblad AB.

## Growth and turning points

Victor had always meant to build a camera for everyone else. On 6 October 1948 he showed the [[hasselblad-1600f|Hasselblad 1600F]] at a press conference in New York. It was a single-lens reflex taking square 6×6 cm pictures on [[film-120|120 film]], and it was built as a system: lenses, film magazines and viewfinders could all be changed, so a photographer could swap from colour to black-and-white in the middle of a roll. The first cameras were fragile, and the improved 1000F followed. In 1952 an American magazine ran 500 rolls through one and dropped it twice without harming it.

The 500C of 1957 made the name. It moved the shutter from the body into the lens, using Zeiss lenses with leaf shutters, so electronic flash worked at every speed. About 76,700 were built by 1970. Portrait and music photographers took it up, and it made many well-known pictures of the 1960s, from film stars to rock bands.

In 1962 the astronaut Walter Schirra, who owned a 500C, persuaded NASA to fly one on his Mercury mission. Stripped of its leather and mirror and painted black, it went round the Earth six times. NASA kept buying Hasselblads, and Hasselblad built cameras for it. On 20 July 1969 Neil Armstrong carried a silver Hasselblad Data Camera on his chest on the Moon, and it took the best-known photographs of the first Moon landing. To save weight, the astronauts left the camera bodies on the Moon and brought home only the film, and by the last Apollo landing twelve Hasselblads had been left there.

## What it made

For decades Hasselblad was the V system: the 500 series and its many versions, the motor-driven 500EL of 1965, the wide-angle Super Wide cameras and later bodies with focal-plane shutters. The modular design outlived the film it was made for. Digital backs could be fitted to old bodies, and the last V-system camera, the 503CWD of 2006, came with one.

Hasselblad also tried new shapes. The XPan of 1998, made with Fujifilm, was a 35mm camera that could take wide 24×65mm panoramas and normal frames on the same roll. In 2002 came the H1, an autofocus medium-format camera. Hasselblad then joined with Imacon, a Danish maker of digital backs and scanners, and its H cameras became digital. In 2016 the X1D brought a sensor larger than [[full-frame-digital|full frame]] into a small mirrorless body.

## Today

Victor Hasselblad sold the company to the Swedish investment firm Säfveån in the late 1970s. He died in 1978 and left most of his fortune to the Erna and Victor Hasselblad Foundation, which awards the Hasselblad Award, one of the best-known prizes in photography. The company was listed on the Stockholm stock exchange in 1984 and has changed owners several times since. The Chinese drone maker [[m:dji|DJI]] bought a stake in 2015 and has held a majority since 2017. Hasselblad still makes its cameras in Gothenburg, and its X cameras borrow the square-and-circle look of the old V system.
