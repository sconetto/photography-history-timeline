## Beginnings

Sony began in the ruins of postwar Tokyo. Masaru Ibuka, an engineer who had worked on military electronics, was repairing radios in a bomb-damaged department store when Akio Morita, a former naval officer he had met during the war, found him again. In May 1946 they started a company with a little borrowed money and called it Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo, Tokyo Telecommunications Engineering. Its first consumer product, a rice cooker, failed.

Electronics went better. In 1950 the company sold Japan's first tape recorder, and in 1955 it began making transistor radios. It sold them under a short name it had made up, Sony, from the Latin *sonus*, "sound", which was easy to say in any language. The name sold so well abroad that in January 1958 the company took it as its own and became the Sony Corporation.

## Growth and turning points

Through the 1960s and 1970s Sony made its name with small, new kinds of electronics: transistor televisions, the Trinitron colour TV of 1968, videotape recorders, and the Walkman of 1979. Pictures, for Sony, meant video, and that led it to the sensor that would change photography.

In spring 1970 Sony began research on the [[ccd|CCD]], a chip that turns light into an electric charge. Early devices were tiny: one in 1972 had 8 × 8 pixels. By 1978 it had a practical sensor of about 120,000 pixels, and in January 1980 it shipped the XC-1, which it calls the first colour video camera to use a CCD. Airliners used it to show take-off on cabin screens.

In 1981 Sony showed the [[mavica|Mavica]], short for Magnetic Video Camera. It looked like an [[g:slr|SLR]] and took interchangeable lenses, but where the film should have been was a CCD, and it stored up to 50 frames on a small floppy disk to be shown on a TV. The pictures were analog and coarse, but every camera company now had to think about photography without film. Sony made later still-video Mavicas for news and industry, and in 1985 its CCD-V8 camcorder put a CCD in a home video camera. That same year Sony began selling its CCDs to other companies.

## What it made

Digital cameras came next. The Cyber-shot DSC-F1 of 1996, Sony's first digital compact, had a lens that swivelled to point forwards, down or back at the photographer. The Digital Mavicas of 1997 saved pictures on ordinary 3.5-inch floppy disks that went straight into a computer, and they became some of the first digital cameras to sell in large numbers.

Just as important were the sensors Sony sold to everyone else. It became the largest maker of CCDs, and its chips went into cameras of many brands. In 2004 it decided to stop expanding CCD production and put its money into [[cmos-sensor|CMOS]] sensors instead. It brought in faster CMOS designs in 2007, back-illuminated sensors that gather more light in 2009, and [[stacked-sensor|stacked sensors]], with the processing circuits built on a second layer, in 2012. In 2011 it became the world's largest image sensor maker again, and its sensors sit in many phones as well as in cameras from other makers.

## Today

In 2006 Sony became a full camera maker. It took over parts of [[m:konica-minolta|Konica Minolta]]'s camera business, including the rights to the lens mount [[m:minolta|Minolta]] had used for its autofocus SLRs since 1985. In June 2006 it announced its first digital SLR, the α100. The name came from Minolta: its SLRs had been sold as "α" (Alpha) in Japan. The α100 kept Minolta's sensor-shift [[image-stabilisation|stabilisation]] and worked with most Maxxum lenses.

Sony then moved away from the mirror. In 2013 the [[sony-a7|α7]] and α7R became the first [[g:full-frame|full-frame]] [[g:mirrorless|mirrorless]] cameras with interchangeable lenses. In 2017 the α9 used a stacked full-frame sensor to shoot 20 frames a second with no blackout in the viewfinder, a challenge to the professional SLRs of Canon and Nikon. Both followed Sony into full-frame mirrorless in 2018.

Sony is now the second-largest camera brand in the world, and through its sensors it is inside far more cameras than the ones with its name on them.
