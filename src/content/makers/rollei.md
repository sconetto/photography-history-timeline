## Beginnings

Rollei began with an idea that its inventor's employer did not want. Reinhold Heidecke was a designer at [[m:voigtlander|Voigtländer]] in Braunschweig, and he had a plan for a new kind of roll-film camera; Voigtländer, which was making good money from plate cameras, turned it down. Paul Franke, a Voigtländer colleague with a head for business, joined him as partner and investor. On 1 February 1920 their workshop for precision mechanics and optics, Franke & Heidecke, was entered in the German trade register.

Their first camera, in 1921, was the Heidoscop, a stereo camera with two Zeiss Tessar lenses for taking pairs of pictures that looked three-dimensional in a viewer. It sold well. A version for roll film followed, and from 1926 it was sold as the Rolleidoscop: the first time the name Rollei was used. Germany's hyperinflation of the early 1920s ruined many firms, but Franke's money sense carried the company through.

## Growth and turning points

Heidecke's real aim was a reflex camera for roll film that was small, tough and quick enough for press photographers. A prototype was ready in 1927, and after a small first batch at the end of 1928 the [[rolleiflex|Rolleiflex]] went on sale in 1929. It had two matched lenses, one above the other: the top one showed the picture on a ground-glass screen, and the bottom one took it, so the photographer could focus and frame right up to the moment of the shot. Orders far outran what the small works could make, even though the camera was expensive, and in 1930 the company moved into a larger factory that could build 20,000 cameras a year.

New models followed quickly. The Baby Rolleiflex of 1931 made smaller pictures on 127 film. From 1932 the Rolleiflex took [[film-120|120 film]] for twelve 6 × 6 cm pictures a roll, and in 1933 the Rolleicord brought the same idea at less than half the price. The Rolleiflex Automat won a Grand Prix at the Paris World's Fair of 1937, and by 1938 Franke & Heidecke had made its 300,000th camera.

During the Second World War the company was made to serve the war economy: it made precision optics for binoculars, periscopes, gun sights and surveying instruments, and almost no new cameras were designed. Braunschweig was an arms-making centre and was bombed heavily; by 1945 about two-thirds of Rollei's factories and machines had been destroyed.

## What it made

Braunschweig was in the British zone, and the company rebuilt slowly, short of materials. Paul Franke died suddenly in March 1950, and his son Horst took his place. For the next decade the Rolleiflex had no real rival. In the 1950s almost every press photographer used one, and so did portrait and fashion photographers such as Richard Avedon and street photographers such as Vivian Maier. The millionth Rollei camera was made in 1956. Hundreds of copies appeared, many of them from Japan; the best, from [[m:mamiya|Mamiya]], offered interchangeable lenses, and Rollei answered with the Tele Rolleiflex of 1959 and the wide-angle Rolleiwide of 1961.

But the market was moving on. Reinhold Heidecke died in 1960. Press and amateur photographers were turning to 35 mm cameras, and studio photographers to [[m:hasselblad|Hasselblad]]'s single-lens reflexes, with their interchangeable lenses and film backs. Rollei's own answer, the Rolleiflex SL66 of 1966, was a fine camera but came years late.

The same year brought a hit. Heinz Waaske had designed a tiny camera for full-size 35 mm pictures at another firm, which then gave up making cameras; he moved to Rollei, and his design was launched at Photokina in 1966 as the Rollei 35, then the smallest 35 mm camera on the market. About two million were made. In 1970 Rollei brought out its first 35 mm SLR, the SL35, and opened a large factory in Singapore, which from 1971 made the Rollei 35 too. In the 1970s it also took over the Voigtländer name, and its slide projectors sold well for nearly twenty years.

## Legacy

Rollei had grown too fast. By 1974 it was carrying half a billion marks of debt, and its 35 mm SLRs could not match Canon, Nikon, Minolta or Pentax. It stopped making twin-lens reflexes in 1979, and in 1981 the company went bankrupt; the Singapore factory closed. A new company, Rollei Fototechnic, carried on in Braunschweig with medium-format SLRs such as the Rolleiflex 6008 of 1988, and the twin-lens Rolleiflex came back in 1987. In 2002 the Rolleiflex 6008 AF was the first 6 × 6 cm camera with autofocus.

From the late 1980s the company changed owners again and again. In 2009 the Braunschweig camera works, then trading as Franke & Heidecke, went insolvent. Three of its former staff set up DHW Fototechnik, which went on making Rolleiflex twin-lens cameras and the Rollei 35 in Braunschweig until it too went insolvent in 2014; its stock was sold in 2015. The Rollei name itself had gone its own way: since 2010 it has belonged to a Hamburg company, called Rollei GmbH & Co. KG since 2015, which sells lenses and other photo and video gear. The Rolleiflex remains one of the best-loved cameras of the 20th century.
