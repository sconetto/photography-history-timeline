## Beginnings

Hoya is named after a place. In November 1941 two brothers, Shoichi and Shigeru Yamanaka, set up a plant to make optical glass in the town of Hoya, in Tokyo. Optical glass is the clear, carefully controlled glass that lenses for cameras, binoculars and microscopes are made from, and Hoya calls itself Japan's first specialist maker of it. The plant became a company in August 1944, with 1.2 million yen of capital.

After the war Hoya turned its glass skills to things people could buy. In October 1945 it began making crystal glassware, a business it kept until 2009.

## Growth and turning points

From the 1960s Hoya moved into one optical business after another. It listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange in 1961, launched progressive multifocal spectacle lenses in 1967 and soft contact lenses in 1972. In 1974 it started making substrates for integrated circuits, and in 1984 it took the name Hoya Corporation. In 1987 it added intraocular lenses, which replace the lens of the eye in cataract surgery, and aspherical lenses made by moulding glass rather than grinding it. Glass disks for computer hard drives followed in 1991.

Photographers also know the name from camera filters, the glass discs screwed onto the front of a lens. With Hoya's approval, the Japanese accessory maker Kenko Tokina sells Hoya-brand filters around the world.

## What it made

In 2007 Hoya became, for a few years, the owner of one of Japan's oldest camera makers. In December 2006 Hoya and [[m:pentax|Pentax]] announced plans to merge. In April 2007 Pentax's board turned against the deal and removed its president, but its largest shareholder still backed it, and in June Hoya made a tender offer of 770 yen a share, valuing Pentax at about 105 billion yen. In August Pentax became a Hoya subsidiary, and the two companies merged in March 2008. Hoya's president said he saw no need for major change to Pentax's cameras.

Pentax kept its name and kept making cameras. Under Hoya it launched the K-m of 2008, which Pentax calls the smallest digital [[g:slr|SLR]] with an APS-C sensor; the weather-sealed K-7 and the brightly coloured K-x of 2009; the 645D of 2010, its first medium-format digital SLR; and the Pentax Q of 2011, a tiny [[g:mirrorless|mirrorless]] camera with its own lens mount.

## Today

Hoya kept the cameras for only four years. In July 2011 it agreed to sell the Pentax imaging business, which included digital cameras, lenses, binoculars and security cameras, to [[m:ricoh|Ricoh]], for a reported 124 million US dollars. The sale took effect on 1 October 2011. Hoya kept the rest of what it had bought: endoscopes, camera modules, optical pickup lenses for DVD players, artificial bone and speech synthesis.

Hoya now makes spectacle and contact lenses, intraocular lenses, optical glass, photomasks for making chips and displays, and medical endoscopes, which it still sells under the name Pentax Medical. The Pentax cameras live on at Ricoh Imaging, and Hoya's name still appears on camera filters around the world.
