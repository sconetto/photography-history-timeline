## Beginnings

The Weston company is named after Edward Weston, an English-born electrical engineer, not the American photographer of the same name. Born in Shropshire in 1850, he came to New York in 1870, worked as a chemist and electrician, and set up a nickel-plating business in 1872. He then built dynamos, the generators that powered the first electric lights. In 1878 he put an arc lamp on the fire department's watchtower in Newark, New Jersey, and by 1886 he held 186 patents.

In all this work Weston was held back by the electrical measuring instruments of the day, which were clumsy and slow. So he built better ones for himself, and they worked so well that in 1888 he gave up his other businesses to make them. He founded the Weston Electrical Instrument Company, with its factory in Newark. Its first products were portable instruments that read electrical current directly from a needle on a dial, the basis of the modern voltmeter, ammeter and wattmeter.

## Growth and turning points

Weston instruments became known for their accuracy. The Weston standard cell, developed in 1893, gave a voltage so steady that an international conference made it the standard for electromotive force in 1908, and laboratories went on using it to calibrate other meters for almost a century. The company made meters of every kind, for power stations, laboratories, radios, aircraft, trains and cars. A British Weston company followed a year after the American one.

The step into photography began with light. Around 1930 Weston was making photoelectric cells, sold as Photronic cells, which turned light into a small electric current that a sensitive needle could measure. In 1928 or 1929 Weston and his son, Edward Faraday Weston, began to experiment with using the cells to measure light for photographs. Edward Faraday Weston applied for a patent on the first Weston exposure meter in 1931.

## What it made

In 1932 the company brought out the Weston Model 617, one of the first photoelectric [[light-meter|exposure meters]]. Its two cells needed no battery: light falling on them moved the needle, and a dial turned the reading into a choice of aperture and shutter speed. It cost $40. The cells were coated and sealed against moisture, which ruins this kind of cell, so many Weston meters still work today. Hollywood used Weston meters too: a Weston foot-candle meter was used in lighting *Gone with the Wind* in 1939.

At first there were no agreed film speeds, so Weston published its own. A photographer looked up the Weston rating of their film and set it on the meter. Weston speeds were widely used until the ASA and DIN scales, the forerunners of today's ISO speeds, took their place, and later Weston meters were marked in those.

Weston's best-known meters were the Masters. The first came out in 1939, and the Master II of 1946 could be fitted with the Invercone, a white plastic dome over the cell. With the dome the meter measured the light falling on the subject, not the light reflected from it, so a dark coat or a white wall would not fool it. From 1932 to the late 1960s Weston sold more than 36 kinds of exposure meter around the world.

## Legacy

Edward Weston died in 1936 with more than 300 patents. In 1954 the American company was bought by Daystrom, which Schlumberger took over in 1962. Weston meters went on: the Master III, IV and V followed, the last American meters were made in 1974, and in England Sangamo Weston made the Euromaster until 1980. Other small companies then kept it in production into the 1980s.

By then most cameras had meters of their own, measuring the light through the lens. But hand-held meters are still used in studios, on film sets and by film photographers, and an old Weston Master with its Invercone is still a working tool for many of them.
