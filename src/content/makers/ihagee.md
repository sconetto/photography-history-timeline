## Beginnings

Ihagee was founded in Dresden on 13 May 1912 by Johan Steenbergen, a young Dutch merchant who had trained at the Ernemann camera works in the same city. Its name came from the German initials of Industrie- und Handels-Gesellschaft, "Industrial and Trading Company", spoken letter by letter: I-Ha-Ge. Steenbergen's mother put up the money, and he bought machines and parts from a small camera maker that had gone bankrupt. The first workshop was in a back yard on Marcolinistraße.

Dresden was then the centre of the German camera industry, and Ihagee began like many of its neighbours, with folding cameras for glass plates and roll film. The First World War and its aftermath hit the young firm hard: by 1918 it was in serious trouble, and it was wound up and started again.

## Growth and turning points

In the 1920s Ihagee grew into one of Dresden's big camera makers, with a long list of folding cameras, reflex boxes and stereo cameras. By 1925 it was making about 1,000 roll-film cameras a day. In 1923 a 19-year-old mechanic named Karl Nüchterlein joined the company. He had no formal technical training, but he became its leading designer.

In 1930 Nüchterlein began work on a small single-lens reflex for 127 roll film. The Exakta was shown at the Leipzig Spring Fair in 1933, and was met with some doubt. It had a trapezoid metal body, a focal-plane shutter up to 1/1000 s, and a mirror behind the lens that showed the picture on a screen on top. Three years later, in March 1936, Nüchterlein brought five samples of a smaller model to the Leipzig fair: the [[kine-exakta|Kine Exakta]], made for 35 mm cine film. It was one of the first 35 mm [[g:slr|SLRs]], and could see exactly what any lens saw, close-up or long, which a rangefinder camera like the [[m:leica|Leica]] could not. Visitors were not sure about it at first, but it became Ihagee's most famous camera.

The Second World War ended this. Civilian cameras stopped by 1940, and the factory was turned over to war production. Steenbergen, the Dutch consul in Dresden, was arrested in May 1940 when Germany invaded the Netherlands, and refused to put his factory at the service of the war effort. His property was confiscated in 1941 and a Nazi party member was made managing director. Steenbergen's wife, an American of Jewish origin, obtained an exit permit, and in May 1942 the couple left for the United States, never to return to Dresden. Nüchterlein was called up in 1942 and went missing in action in 1944. On 13 and 14 February 1945 the bombing of Dresden burned out Ihagee's main factory.

## What it made

The survivors of the workforce salvaged what they could and started making Exaktas again in 1945, in a rented building on Blasewitzer Straße. Dresden was now in the Soviet zone, which became East Germany. Most of its camera makers were nationalised, but Ihagee was a foreign-owned company, and for years it kept an unusual degree of independence under state trustees.

In 1950 it launched the Exakta Varex, the first Exakta with interchangeable finders: a waist-level hood, or a [[pentaprism]] for viewing at eye level. The American camera maker [[m:argus|Argus]] had registered the name Varex in the US, so cameras for that market were labelled VX. Its range of lenses and accessories made the Exakta popular for scientific and close-up work, and in Alfred Hitchcock's film *Rear Window* (1954) the photographer played by James Stewart uses one. In 1951 came the Exa, a cheaper, simpler SLR that took the same lenses. Ihagee made no lenses of its own; Carl Zeiss Jena, Meyer, Schneider and many others made lenses for the Exakta mount. In 1959 Ihagee built nearly 73,000 cameras, about 70% of them for export.

## Legacy

Ihagee's shareholders, unsure of the firm's future, moved its registered office to Frankfurt in 1959. From 1960 there were two Ihagees, one in each Germany, and they fought each other in court for years. The western company later sold Exakta cameras made by other firms, including Japanese makers such as Petri and Cosina.

In Dresden, the state-owned VEB Pentacon was formed in 1964 and took over more and more of Ihagee's work: first its development department, then, from 1968, its sales, advertising and service. The Exakta VX 1000 of 1967 and the cheaper VX 500 of 1969 were the last of the line that began with the Kine Exakta. In 1970 the Ihagee buildings and machines were leased to the Pentacon combine, and Ihagee stopped being an independent camera maker; the company was struck off the register in 1971. Pentacon kept the Exakta and Exa names for its own cameras for years afterwards.

Steenbergen became a Dutch consul again after the war and died in 1967. The Exakta's place in history is secure: it showed that the 35 mm single-lens reflex could work, years before the Japanese makers made it the camera of choice.
