## Beginnings

Alfred Hugh Harman was a photographer with a business in Peckham, in south London. In 1879 he began making [[gelatin-dry-plate|gelatin dry plates]] in the basement of his house in Cranbrook Road, in the town of Ilford, east of London. Dry plates could be made in a factory, kept for months and developed after the picture was taken, and they were quickly replacing the messy wet collodion process. Harman's plates sold well, and in 1880 he moved to bigger premises, trading as the Britannia Works Company. A factory followed in 1883.

Harman sold his interest in 1897 because of ill health. The company later wanted to take the name of its town, and in 1901, after objections from the local council, it became Ilford, Limited. Twice, in 1897 and 1903, Eastman [[m:kodak|Kodak]] tried to take it over, without success.

## Growth and turning points

Ilford came late to roll film, but in 1920 it joined other British makers in Selo, a joint company for making roll film, and by 1925 Ilford controlled it. Around 1928 it bought several smaller British photographic firms, among them Rajar, whose factory at Mobberley, in Cheshire, became the home of Ilford's photographic paper. In 1931 it introduced its Hypersensitive Panchromatic plates, sensitive to all colours, the start of the HP films. In 1940 it launched Multigrade, the first variable-contrast paper, which caught on after it was relaunched in 1953.

After the Second World War Ilford tried colour films and cameras, including the Witness rangefinder, of which only about 350 were made. In the late 1950s the chemical company ICI bought a large stake, and from 1963 the Swiss chemical company Ciba bought shares until, in 1969, it owned Ilford outright. Ciba brought Cibachrome, a process for printing colour slides straight onto paper, which Ilford later sold as Ilfochrome. Ciba also owned [[m:lumiere|Lumière]] in Lyon, which became Ilford France in 1982.

## What it made

Ilford is best known for black-and-white film and paper. FP4, a fine-grain film, came out in 1968, and HP5, a fast ISO 400 film, followed in 1976. Their Plus versions of 1989 and 1990 are still made. In 1974 Ilford brought out Ilfospeed resin-coated papers, which developed and dried much faster than fibre papers, and in 1980 XP1, a black-and-white film that could be developed in ordinary colour-negative chemicals. Many darkroom photographers learned on Ilford film and paper.

## Legacy

Ciba sold Ilford to the American company International Paper in 1989, and in 1997 it was sold again, to investors, and the next year renamed Ilford Imaging. Digital cameras then cut demand for film quickly. In 2004 the British company went into administration. Six of its managers bought the black-and-white business at Mobberley, and in 2005 they formed [[m:harman|Harman Technology]], named after Ilford's founder, which still makes Ilford film and paper there. The Ilford name on other products, such as inkjet paper, went separately to other owners.
