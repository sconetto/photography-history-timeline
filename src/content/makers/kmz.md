## Beginnings

KMZ began in the middle of the Second World War. It was founded in 1942 at Krasnogorsk, a suburb west of Moscow, from an optical plant evacuated ahead of the German advance; one account says it started in Sverdlovsk, in the Urals, and moved to Krasnogorsk in 1944. Its name means the Krasnogorsk Mechanical Works, and its first job was optical equipment for the Red Army.

At the end of the war the Red Army captured the [[m:zeiss|Carl Zeiss]] works at Jena, and from 1945 KMZ made lenses to specifications taken from it. In 1946 it made its first camera, the Moskva, a folding camera for roll film based on a pre-war Zeiss Ikon model.

## Growth and turning points

In 1948 KMZ began building the FED, the Soviet copy of the [[m:leica|Leica]] II, because the FED factory had been damaged in the war and could not make enough. When FED recovered, KMZ kept making its own version under the name Zorki, meaning sharp-sighted, and over the next thirty years its Zorki rangefinders moved away from the Leica design. The Zorki 4 alone was made from 1956 to 1973.

In 1952 KMZ turned the Zorki into a single-lens reflex by adding a mirror box, and called it the Zenit. Zenit became the name of all its SLRs, and in time of the whole factory. More than 780,000 of the Zenit-3M of 1962 were made, many of them sold abroad. The Zenit-E of 1965 was the big one: a plain, heavy SLR with a built-in light meter, an [[instant-return-mirror|instant-return mirror]] and, from 1967, the common 42mm screw mount. It was cheap, and for many people in Europe it was their first SLR. Counting all its versions, eight to twelve million may have been made, which would make it one of the most-produced SLRs ever.

## What it made

Besides Zorkis and Zenits, KMZ made a long list of lenses with names such as Helios, Jupiter and Industar, many of them still used today. It made the Horizont of 1967, a panoramic camera whose lens swung across the scene, and in its special workshop it made cameras for the KGB and other intelligence services: tiny subminiature cameras, a camera hidden behind a coat button, and the Photosniper, a Zenit outfit with a long telephoto lens. Many KMZ cameras were also sold in the West under shop brands, such as the Horizont as the Revue.

## Today

After the Soviet Union ended, the factory was privatised in 1993 as the S. A. Zverev Krasnogorsky Zavod, but kept the Zenit brand. Film cameras could not compete, and in 2005 KMZ closed its camera division, keeping only the Horizon panoramic cameras. Since 2008 it has been part of Shvabe, the optics arm of the Russian state corporation Rostec, and it mostly makes military, space and surveying optics. It began making Zenitar and Helios lenses for photographers again in the 2010s, and in 2018, at Photokina, it showed the Zenit M, a digital rangefinder built with Leica, the company whose camera the Zorki had first copied.
