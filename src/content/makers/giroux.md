## Beginnings

The first company to sell a camera was not a camera maker at all. Maison Alphonse Giroux was founded in Paris in 1799 by François-Simon-Alphonse Giroux, an art restorer and cabinetmaker, in a shop at 7 rue du Coq-Saint-Honoré. At first it sold artists' supplies alongside furniture Giroux made himself. It soon moved into luxury goods of every kind, from decorated boxes and bindings to furniture and works of art, and French kings and members of the royal family became its customers.

Giroux's sons, André and Alphonse-Gustave, joined the business in 1834, the year the firm won a silver medal at the national exhibition of French industry for its richly decorated bindings. When their father retired in 1838, Alphonse-Gustave carried on the firm as Alphonse Giroux et Cie.

## Growth and turning points

The Giroux family was related by marriage to Louis Daguerre, who had spent years developing the [[daguerreotype]] with Nicéphore Niépce and, after Niépce's death, with his son Isidore. In 1839 the French state bought the process and gave it to the world. Daguerre and Isidore Niépce signed a contract that gave Giroux the right to make and sell the equipment, and when the process was published in August 1839, the shop was ready.

Daguerre designed the camera himself: a sliding wooden box with a Chevalier [[achromatic-lens|achromatic lens]]. Giroux built it in a series and sold it with everything else needed to make a picture, and every genuine camera carried a seal with Daguerre's signature. Within months Giroux cameras were being sent abroad. Other Paris makers, such as Susse Frères, soon sold their own outfits, and William Henry Fox Talbot, photography's other inventor, owned a [[giroux-camera|Giroux camera]] that he adapted for his own paper process.

## What it made

The Giroux camera was big and slow, built for the daguerreotype's long exposures, and within two years smaller cameras with the much faster [[petzval-lens|Petzval lens]] had overtaken it. Giroux's time as a camera maker lasted only a few years, but its main business carried on. In 1855 Empress Eugénie bought a carved writing desk from it at the Paris Exposition Universelle, and in 1857 the shop moved to large new galleries on the boulevard des Capucines.

## Legacy

In 1867 the family handed the business to Ferdinand Duvinage and his partner, who kept the Giroux name. Under Duvinage and later his widow, the shop became known for inlays of ivory and brass, and it closed in the 1880s. Its few surviving daguerreotype cameras are now among the most valuable cameras ever sold at auction, and they mark the moment photography became something anyone with money could buy in a shop.
