## Beginnings

Zeiss began, like Leitz, with microscopes. In 1846 Carl Zeiss opened a workshop for precision mechanics and optical instruments in Jena, in Thuringia, and soon concentrated on microscopes, which he sold far beyond the region. Good lenses were then made by trial and error: a craftsman ground them, tested them and tried again.

In 1866 Zeiss hired a young physicist, Ernst Abbe, to put the work on a scientific footing, and Abbe later became his partner. Abbe worked out how to calculate a lens before it was made, and he needed glasses that did not yet exist. The chemist Otto Schott made them, and their glassworks opened in Jena in 1884. Together they turned lens making into [[scientific-lens-design|scientific lens design]], and lenses calculated from theory, made from glass of known properties, became the rule.

Carl Zeiss died at the end of 1888. In 1889 Abbe set up the Carl Zeiss Foundation, which became the company's sole owner. Its profits went to science, to social and cultural projects and to the workforce.

## Growth and turning points

From the 1890s Abbe's methods spread to new kinds of optics, among them camera lenses. In 1890 Zeiss began making the Anastigmat, later called the Protar, designed by the scientist Paul Rudolph, which Zeiss calls the first anastigmatic lens, free of distortion. Rudolph followed it with the prototype of the Planar in 1896 and the [[tessar|Tessar]] in 1902. Zeiss calls the Tessar the most famous and most copied camera lens ever made.

Zeiss also went into cameras. In 1926, with Zeiss as majority owner, four German camera makers, Ica, Contessa-Nettel, Ernemann and Goerz, merged to form Zeiss Ikon in Dresden. Zeiss Ikon made folding cameras such as the Ikonta and, in 1932, the Contax, a 35 mm rangefinder built to beat the Leica. Its fast Sonnar lenses made the 35 mm camera useful in far more kinds of light. In 1935 Alexander Smakula at Zeiss developed a thin [[lens-coating|anti-reflection coating]] for glass, the T coating, which cut flare and later let designers build lenses with many more elements.

During the First World War Zeiss made binoculars, rangefinders and other optics for the army. Under the Nazi regime it made precision instruments that went into weapons, and it used forced labour. Zeiss later took part in Germany's "Remembrance, Responsibility and Future" foundation, set up to compensate forced labourers.

## What it made

The Second World War split Zeiss in two. In 1945 the American troops leaving Thuringia took 77 of its leading scientists and engineers with them, and these people founded a new company in Oberkochen, in the West. The works in Jena, in the Soviet zone, were nationalised in 1948. Machines from Zeiss Ikon's Dresden factories went to the Soviet Union as reparations, and the Contax line was rebuilt there as the Kiev. In Dresden the East German Zeiss Ikon brought out the [[pentaprism|Contax S]], one of the first 35 mm SLRs with an eye-level pentaprism. The two Zeiss companies stopped working together in 1953 and fought over the name around the world until 1989.

In the West, Zeiss Ikon moved to Stuttgart and later merged with [[m:voigtlander|Voigtländer]], which the Zeiss Foundation had controlled since 1956. Zeiss lenses travelled further than ever. The Planar 50 mm f/0.7 of 1966 was, according to Zeiss, the fastest lens in the world, and Zeiss lenses on Hasselblad cameras took the photographs of the first Moon landing in 1969.

But the cameras could not compete with Japan. Zeiss Ikon stopped making cameras in 1972, a shock for the whole German camera industry. Some of its products went to [[m:rollei|Rollei]], and the Contax name was revived in Japan: Zeiss joined a group with the Japanese maker Yashica that brought out the Contax RTS SLR in 1974, and from 1983 Zeiss worked with Kyocera, which by then owned Yashica.

## Today

After the Berlin Wall fell, the East and West companies merged again in 1991, and the reunited firm went through a hard crisis in the mid-1990s, with deep cuts to its workforce and products. In 2004 the company became a stock company, Carl Zeiss AG, owned entirely by the Carl Zeiss Foundation. It is based in Oberkochen and still makes optics in Jena.

Zeiss no longer sells cameras of its own, but its lenses are everywhere. A partnership with [[m:sony|Sony]] began with a camcorder lens in 1996, and in 1999 Zeiss made its first lens for a consumer digital camera, the Sony DSC-F55E. In 2004 it revived the Zeiss Ikon name for a rangefinder camera made by Cosina in Japan. Zeiss makes lenses for Nikon and Canon SLR mounts, cinema lenses that have won three Academy Awards, and phone camera optics. Its phone partnership began with Nokia in 2005 and continued with vivo from 2020. Most of Zeiss's business today lies outside photography, in fields such as medical technology and optics for making computer chips, but the blue Zeiss badge still marks lenses for photographers around the world.
