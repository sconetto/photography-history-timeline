## Beginnings

Kazuo Tashima worked for his father's trading company in Kobe. On a trip to Europe he was struck by what optical instruments could do, and on 11 November 1928 he founded Nichi-Doku Shashinki Shōten, the "Japan-Germany Camera Company". The name reflected the plan: to build cameras in Japan with German know-how. There were no suppliers nearby, so engineers invited from Germany guided the making of each small part in the company's own factory in what is now Nishinomiya.

The first camera, the Nifcalette, came out in 1929. The Minolta name followed on the company's cameras in the 1930s, and the business grew into Minolta Camera Co. It made its own lenses, sold as Rokkor, and in the 1950s it was one of the first Japanese companies to show its cameras at trade fairs in the United States.

## Growth and turning points

Tashima loved to watch the stars. After visiting Japan's first planetarium, he set out to build one, working with an amateur inventor, Masasuke Nobuoka, and the company's lens designers. Their projector opened to the public at a science exposition in 1958, and over 200,000 people came in three months. Planetariums became a lasting Minolta business.

The same year brought Minolta's first [[g:slr|single-lens reflex]], the SR-2. Its cameras soon reached space. John Glenn photographed the Earth with a Minolta Hi-Matic on Friendship 7 in 1962, and a Minolta exposure meter, the Space Meter, went to the Moon on Apollo 11. The company says Glenn's pictures changed how the world saw Japanese cameras.

Through the 1960s and 1970s Minolta made SLRs for enthusiasts, rangefinder compacts and, from 1960, copiers. Its SLRs added automation step by step. The XD of 1977 offered both aperture-priority and shutter-priority exposure in one camera.

Then came the turn that defined Minolta. In February 1985 it launched the α-7000, sold as the Maxxum 7000 in the United States. Earlier [[autofocus|autofocus]] cameras had been compacts, or SLRs with a motor in a special lens. The 7000 was a whole SLR system built for autofocus: sensors and motor in the body, a new lens mount and a new range of lenses. Konica Minolta says it "enjoyed explosive sales worldwide" and that rivals followed with autofocus SLRs of their own. [[m:canon|Canon]] answered with a new mount; [[m:nikon|Nikon]] kept its old one.

Success brought a lawsuit. Honeywell, the American company whose autofocus modules had gone into cameras such as [[m:konica|Konica]]'s, said Minolta had used its patents. In February 1992 a US jury agreed in part and ordered Minolta to pay $96 million, and Honeywell went on to pursue other camera makers over the same patents.

## What it made

Minolta made SLRs, compacts and lenses for 75 years, but it was never only a camera company. Its planetarium projectors, colour meters for television, light meters and copiers grew into large businesses. In 1994 it dropped "Camera" from its name and became Minolta Co., Ltd.

Its best-loved cameras were the ones that made photography easier: the Hi-Matic, the XD, the Maxxum 7000 and the long line of Maxxum (Dynax in Europe, α in Japan) autofocus SLRs that followed.

## Legacy

By 2000 Minolta and Konica were already working together on office equipment, where both had large copier businesses. In August 2003 the two merged into [[m:konica-minolta|Konica Minolta]]. The merged company made the first digital SLR with a sensor that moved to cancel camera shake, in 2004, but left cameras altogether in 2006. Minolta's autofocus mount, the A mount of 1985, passed to [[m:sony|Sony]], which used it to start its own SLR line. The α name Minolta gave its cameras in Japan lives on in Sony's cameras today.
