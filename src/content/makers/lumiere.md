## Beginnings

Antoine Lumière was a painter who became a photographer. He opened a studio in Besançon in 1860 and in 1871 moved to Lyon, where he won medals for his portraits. His sons Auguste, born in 1862, and Louis, born in 1864, grew up in the studio and studied science at La Martinière, Lyon's technical school.

At the time, photographers were switching from wet collodion to [[gelatin-dry-plate|gelatin dry plates]], which could be made in a factory and kept until needed. While still a student, at about 17, Louis worked out a better, faster dry plate. With their father's money the brothers began making it by hand in 1882 and selling it through the family studio, and the next year the family set up a company to make plates at Monplaisir, on the edge of Lyon.

## Growth and turning points

Louis's plate, sold as the Étiquette bleue for its blue label, was a huge success. Output rose from a few thousand plates a year to more than a million in 1886 and 15 million in 1894, when the Monplaisir factory employed about 300 people. The plates paid for everything the brothers did next.

In 1894 Antoine saw Edison's Kinetoscope, a peep-show machine for moving pictures, and urged his sons to do better. Louis designed a hand-cranked box that could take films, print them and project them, the Cinématographe, patented in February 1895. Its first film showed workers leaving the Lumière factory. On 28 December 1895 the Lumières showed their films to a paying audience in a café basement in Paris, a show often counted as the birth of cinema. Lumière camera operators were soon filming all over the world, but by 1905 the brothers had left the film business to others.

## What it made

Their next goal was colour. In 1904 the brothers presented the [[autochrome|Autochrome]] to the French Academy of Sciences, and a new factory was built for it. Making the plates was an industrial feat: potato starch was sieved to keep only grains 10 to 15 thousandths of a millimetre across, the grains were dyed red, green and violet, mixed and spread on glass, gaps were filled with charcoal powder, the layer was pressed flat under great pressure, and a panchromatic emulsion went on top. Each grain acted as a tiny colour filter, so the developed plate showed the scene in soft, glowing colour.

Autochromes went on sale in 1907 and were shown to the public that June at the offices of the magazine *L'Illustration*. They needed exposures about thirty times longer than black-and-white plates, but for about thirty years they had no real rival, and the factory was soon making thousands a day. Later the process moved from glass to film. The Lumières also invented curiosities such as the Photorama, a camera and projector for 360-degree panoramas, and stereoscopic films.

## Legacy

Louis Lumière died in 1948 and Auguste, who had turned to medical research, in 1954. The company had merged with the plate maker Jougla in 1911 and traded as the Société Lumière. In the 1960s it passed to the Swiss chemical group Ciba, which also came to own the British film maker [[m:ilford|Ilford]], and in 1982 the Lyon business took the name Ilford France.

Little of the Monplaisir factory survives. The family villa beside it is now the Institut Lumière, a museum and cinema on the street named rue du Premier-Film, the street of the first film. The Autochrome, the first colour process ordinary photographers could use, and the Cinématographe both came out of a business built on a better dry plate.
