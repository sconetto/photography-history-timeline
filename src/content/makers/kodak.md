## Beginnings

George Eastman was a young bank clerk in Rochester, New York, and a keen amateur photographer at a time when taking pictures meant hauling a heavy camera, glass plates and a tent full of chemicals. In 1878 he took up the new gelatin dry plates, which could be prepared in advance and developed later. In 1879 he invented a machine for coating them, and in 1880 he began making dry plates for sale in a rented loft.

In January 1881 Eastman and Henry Strong, a family friend who made buggy whips, formed the Eastman Dry Plate Company. By September Eastman had left the bank to run the business full-time. His aim, as he put it, was to make photography "as convenient as the pencil", and the way to do it was to replace the glass plate with something light and flexible. In 1884 he and William Walker invented a roll holder for paper negatives, and in 1885 came Eastman American Film.

## Growth and turning points

In 1888 the name Kodak appeared on a new camera, sold with the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest". The [[kodak-camera|Kodak]] was a small box, already loaded with film for 100 pictures. When the roll was finished, the owner sent the whole camera to Rochester, where the film was developed and printed and the camera reloaded. Snapshot photography was born. In 1889 Eastman's company brought out the first commercial transparent [[roll-film|roll film]], on a clear plastic base, which also made Thomas Edison's motion-picture camera possible. The company became Eastman Kodak in 1892.

Kodak kept making cameras and film easier to use. The Folding Pocket Kodak of 1898 set the pattern for roll-film cameras for decades, and the [[brownie|Brownie]] of 1900 cost one dollar, with film at 15 cents a roll, putting a camera within reach of almost everyone. In 1908 Kodak made the first practical safety film, on a base that did not burn like nitrate, and in 1923 its 16 mm reversal film and Cine-Kodak camera made home movies practical. Kodak grew into one of the world's great industrial companies. In 1912 Eastman hired the British scientist Kenneth Mees to set up one of the first industrial research laboratories in the United States. Kodak made film for the new X-rays, for the movie industry and for the military in both world wars. When Eastman died in 1932 he left his estate to the University of Rochester; his house became the George Eastman Museum.

## What it made

Kodak's research laboratories shaped colour photography. [[kodachrome|Kodachrome]], invented by Leopold Godowsky and Leopold Mannes, arrived in 1935 and became the first commercially successful colour film for amateurs. Kodacolor of 1942 was the first true [[colour-negative-film|colour negative film]] for prints, and Ektachrome followed. Tri-X, a fast black-and-white film, came out in 1954. Until then the price of Kodak colour film had included processing; a court settlement in 1954 ended that, and from 1955 independent labs could develop it, using Kodak chemicals and paper. The Carousel projector of 1961, with a round tray of 80 slides, became the standard way to show slides at home.

Kodak also sold cameras by the million. Its German factory made the Retina 35 mm cameras from 1934, and the Super Kodak Six-20 of 1938 was the first camera with built-in automatic exposure. The [[instamatic|Instamatic]] of 1963 used a drop-in film cartridge, so no one had to thread film again; more than 50 million were made by 1970. The pocket Instamatics of 1972 used the tiny 110 cartridge. A Kodak stereo camera went to the Moon with Apollo 11 in 1969.

Kodak's own engineers also invented much of what came next. In 1975 Steven Sasson built the [[digital-camera-sasson|first digital camera]] in a Kodak lab, and in 1976 Bryce Bayer patented the [[bayer-filter|colour filter]] that almost every digital camera still uses. Kodak made the first [[megapixel-sensor|megapixel sensor]] in 1986, and in 1991 sold the first commercial digital SLR, the [[kodak-dcs|DCS]], a Nikon F3 with a Kodak sensor.

## Today

But Kodak earned its money from film, paper and processing, and it struggled to make digital pay in the same way. It had turned down Edwin Land's instant camera in the 1940s; when it finally brought out its own instant cameras in 1976, [[m:polaroid|Polaroid]] sued for patent infringement. In 1986 the courts ordered Kodak out of instant photography, and in 1991 it paid Polaroid $925 million. Japanese competition from [[m:fujifilm|Fujifilm]] cut into its film sales, and the disc camera of 1982 did not last. Its EasyShare digital cameras were popular in the 2000s, but film sales collapsed, and it retired Kodachrome in 2009.

On 19 January 2012 Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. It stopped making digital cameras, sold its digital imaging patents, and handed its consumer photography and document businesses to its UK pension plan, which runs them as Kodak Alaris. Eastman Kodak emerged from bankruptcy in September 2013 as a much smaller company focused on commercial printing. It still makes still and motion-picture film in Rochester, and with the [[film-revival|film revival]] it brought back Ektachrome slide film in 2018.
