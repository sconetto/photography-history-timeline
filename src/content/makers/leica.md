## Beginnings

Leica began as a maker of microscopes. In 1849 the young optician Carl Kellner founded an Optical Institute in Wetzlar, a small town in Hesse, to make lenses and microscopes. Kellner died in 1855. Ernst Leitz, a mechanic, joined the business in 1864, became a partner the next year and by 1869 was its sole owner. The firm took his name, and under Leitz and later his son, Ernst Leitz II, it grew into a respected maker of microscopes and scientific instruments.

The camera came from an engineer with a hobby. Oskar Barnack joined Leitz in 1911 and was soon running development for cinema and measuring equipment. Before that he had worked at [[m:zeiss|Carl Zeiss]] in Jena, where, according to the company history, a manager turned down his idea for a small camera. By 1913–14 he had built a small camera of his own that used the 35 mm film of the cinema. It ran the film sideways and made each frame twice the size of a cinema frame, 24 × 36 mm. That frame became the [[format-135|35 mm format]] photographers still call "full frame".

The First World War and the inflation that followed held the camera back. Leitz made a small series of test cameras, now called the 0 series, and lent them out to see how they worked in real use. In 1924 Ernst Leitz II decided to build it, in Leica's account with the words "we will take the risk". The [[leica-i|Leica I]], short for *Lei*tz *Ca*mera, was shown at the Leipzig Spring Fair in 1925, with an Elmar lens designed by Max Berek.

## Growth and turning points

The Leica found its users fast. About 57,000 were made between 1925 and 1930. A small, quiet camera with 36 exposures on a roll let photographers work among people, unnoticed, and catch moments as they happened. Photographers such as Henri Cartier-Bresson and Robert Capa made it the camera of a new kind of reportage.

Leitz kept improving it. The Leica II of 1932 was the first Leica with a built-in [[g:rangefinder|rangefinder]] coupled to the lens, and the Leica III followed in 1933. Interchangeable lenses turned it into a system. Its great rival was Zeiss Ikon's Contax, also from 1932. To serve North America, Leitz later set up a subsidiary with a factory in Midland, Canada.

In 1954, at Photokina in Cologne, Leitz showed the [[leica-m3|Leica M3]]. It had a bayonet mount and a large, bright finder that showed the rangefinder and the frame together, and it started the M system that Leica still makes. The M2 of 1957 was a simpler, cheaper version. Ernst Leitz II died in 1956.

## What it made

By then Japanese makers were turning to the [[g:slr|single-lens reflex]], and Leitz followed slowly. Its first SLR, the Leicaflex, came in 1965, followed by the Leicaflex SL in 1968 and the SL2 in 1974. In 1972 Leitz signed a partnership with [[m:minolta|Minolta]], and the Leica R3 of 1976 shared much with a Minolta. Leitz opened a plant in Portugal in 1973. Its M5 rangefinder, with a built-in meter, turned out too bulky, and its extra development costs added to the strain. The M6 of 1984 got it right: it fitted a light meter inside the classic M body, and its shutter still needed no battery.

The company changed shape in the 1980s. Leitz merged with the Swiss optical group Wild Heerbrugg in 1987, and the camera business moved to Solms, near Wetzlar, in 1988. In 1996 Leica Camera AG was listed on the stock exchange in Frankfurt, apart from the microscope and surveying businesses. That year it also launched the R8 film SLR and its first digital camera, the S1, a scanning camera for museums and studios.

## Today

Leica was slow to go digital and by 2004 was in deep trouble. That year Andreas Kaufmann, through an investment company he had set up with his brothers, took a stake in Leica Camera AG. He became its majority shareholder and chairs its supervisory board. Leica's first digital M, the M8 of 2006, used a sensor smaller than full frame. The M9 of 2009 was the first digital rangefinder with a full-frame sensor, so the old M lenses worked at their true angle of view. Later came the M Monochrom, which records only black and white, and the M11 of 2022, with a choice of 60, 36 or 18 megapixels.

Leica also built new systems. The Leica T of 2014 introduced the lens mount now called L-Mount, and the SL of 2015 used it for a full-frame [[g:mirrorless|mirrorless]] camera. In 2018 Leica shared the mount with [[m:panasonic|Panasonic]] and Sigma in the L-Mount Alliance. The Leica Q of 2015 put a full-frame sensor behind a fixed 28 mm lens.

On 23 May 2014, 100 years after Barnack's first camera, Leica moved its headquarters back to Wetzlar, to a new campus called Leitz-Park, with a museum and a gallery open to visitors. It also makes cameras in Vila Nova de Famalicão, Portugal. Leica still sells film cameras, including a new M6 from 2022, and its M11-P is, Leica says, the first camera to add Content Credentials, a signed record that shows a photo is real, in an age of [[generative-ai|AI-made images]].
