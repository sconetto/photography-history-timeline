## Beginnings

Ricoh came out of a research institute. RIKEN, Japan's Institute of Physical and Chemical Research, set up a company called Rikagaku Kogyo in 1927 to sell the products of its laboratories, among them sensitised paper, the light-sensitive paper used to copy drawings. On 6 February 1936 that paper business was split off as Riken Kankoshi Co., Ltd., with 33 staff and 350,000 yen in capital. Kiyoshi Ichimura, who ran it, is counted as Ricoh's founder.

In 1938 the company became Riken Optical Industries, a name that fitted what it now did. Its Oji factory in Tokyo made sensitised paper, but also cameras and binoculars.

## Growth and turning points

After the war Ricoh made cameras for ordinary people. In March 1950 it launched the Ricohflex III, a 6 × 6 cm twin-lens reflex. In 1953 it became the first Japanese company to build cameras on a belt conveyor. Its line could turn out 10,000 cameras a month, when hand assembly managed fewer than 1,000, so it could sell the Ricohflex at a price that seemed remarkably low for what was then a luxury. By Ricoh's account, at its peak the Ricohflex made up more than half of all cameras made in Japan.

Ricoh then led the way in automatic cameras. Its Auto 35 of 1960 was the first Japanese camera with an electric eye, a light cell that set the exposure. In November 1962 came the Auto Half, a tiny half-frame camera that set its own exposure and wound its own film, so anyone could use it. It was a great success.

At the same time Ricoh found the business that would become its main one. Its first office copier, the Ricopy 101 of 1955, was so popular that "to make a Ricopy" came to mean "to make a copy" in Japan. In April 1963, with sales passing 10 billion yen, the company took the name Ricoh Company, Ltd. Copiers, fax machines and office computers followed, and office equipment soon earned far more than cameras.

## What it made

Ricoh kept making cameras, many of them unusual. Its first digital camera, the RDC-1 of 1995, was, by the Digital Camera Museum's account, the first digital camera that could also record video, and its add-on modules included a screen and a wide-angle converter. In October 1996 came the GR1, a film compact built for photographers who wanted a camera they could keep in a pocket without giving up image quality. It had a magnesium body, full control of exposure and a 28mm GR lens of seven elements, sharp across the whole frame. Professionals adopted it, and the GR21 that followed was the first compact with a 21mm lens.

In 2005 the GR DIGITAL carried the idea into digital photography, with a 28mm-equivalent lens, a small CCD sensor and a body much like the GR1's. Ricoh kept adding features through firmware updates long after release, which was unusual then.

## Today

On 1 October 2011 Ricoh took over [[m:pentax|Pentax]]'s camera business from [[m:hoya|Hoya]] and set up Pentax Ricoh Imaging, renamed Ricoh Imaging in August 2013. It combined the Pentax digital [[g:slr|SLRs]] and lenses with Ricoh's own cameras. The GR of 2013 put an APS-C sensor, the size used in many interchangeable-lens cameras, into a pocket-sized body, and Ricoh dropped "DIGITAL" from the name. The GR III of 2019 added a 24-megapixel sensor and stabilisation, and the GR IV followed in 2025. Ricoh calls the GR the "ultimate snapshooter", and the series turns 30 in October 2026.

Ricoh also built new kinds of camera. The RICOH THETA, announced in September 2013, used two back-to-back fisheye lenses to capture a complete sphere in one shot, and Ricoh calls it the first mass-produced device to do so. Under Ricoh Imaging, Pentax launched the Pentax 17 in 2024, a new film camera for the [[film-revival|film revival]]. Ricoh's main business today is office printing and document services, but through Pentax and the GR it still makes cameras for both film and digital photography.
