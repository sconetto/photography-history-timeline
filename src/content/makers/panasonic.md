## Beginnings

Panasonic began in a rented house in Osaka. Konosuke Matsushita, 23, had left his job at the Osaka Electric Light Company to sell a light socket of his own design, but it sold badly, and money was so short the family made trips to the pawnshop. On 7 March 1918 he, his wife Mumeno and her 15-year-old brother Toshio Iue turned the ground floor of their home into a workshop and founded Matsushita Electric Housewares Manufacturing Works.

Its first success was an improved attachment plug, the fitting that let a household appliance draw power from a ceiling light socket. Matsushita made the screw part from the metal bases of used light bulbs, which fitted the socket exactly and cost almost nothing, so his plugs sold for about 30% less than others. Orders poured in. A double socket followed in 1920, a bicycle lamp in 1923, an electric iron in 1927 and a heater in 1929. The company became Matsushita Electric Manufacturing Works in 1929 and Matsushita Electric Industrial in 1935, and in 1933 it moved its head office to Kadoma, outside Osaka.

## Growth and turning points

After the war Matsushita turned his company into one of the giants of consumer electronics. He visited the United States in 1951, and in 1953 the company brought washing machines, televisions and refrigerators to Japanese homes. In 1954 it bought half of the Japan Victor Company, JVC. At home it sold under the brand National; abroad, under Panasonic.

Its biggest bet in pictures was on home video. In the 1970s Matsushita backed JVC's VHS videocassette system against [[m:sony|Sony]]'s Betamax and licensed it to other makers, and VHS won. Panasonic also made video cameras for homes and broadcasters, which gave it decades of experience with image sensors, lenses and moving pictures, but not with still photography.

## What it made

Panasonic came to still cameras late, with digital. In August 2000 it began working with [[m:leica|Leica]] on lenses for digital and audio-visual equipment, and in 2001 the two agreed to cooperate on digital cameras. That autumn came the first cameras with the Lumix name, the DMC-LC5 and DMC-F7, with Leica-branded lenses.

Panasonic also joined the Four Thirds standard, and in 2008 it took the system somewhere new. In September it announced the [[lumix-g1|Lumix G1]], the first camera for Micro Four Thirds, a system Panasonic had developed with [[m:olympus|Olympus]]. The G1 had no reflex mirror. Light went straight from the lens to the sensor, which fed an electronic viewfinder and a swivelling screen, so the camera could be much smaller than a digital [[g:slr|SLR]]. It was the first [[g:mirrorless|mirrorless]] interchangeable-lens camera, the design most cameras now use. That October, Matsushita Electric Industrial renamed itself Panasonic Corporation, and it set out to replace the National brand in Japan with Panasonic by 2010.

## Today

Panasonic built its cameras around video. The GH4 of 2014 was the first mirrorless camera to record 4K video, and the GH5 and later models won Panasonic a following among professional video makers. In 2018 it went beyond Micro Four Thirds: with Leica and Sigma it formed the L-Mount Alliance, sharing the lens mount Leica had designed, and it announced its first [[g:full-frame|full-frame]] cameras, the Lumix S1R and S1. They went on sale in 2019 with stabilisation in both body and lens and 4K video at 60 frames a second.

In 2022 Leica and Panasonic signed a wider agreement to develop cameras, lenses and software together. Panasonic still makes Lumix cameras in both Micro Four Thirds and full frame.
