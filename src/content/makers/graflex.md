## Beginnings

William F. Folmer was an inventor, and with William E. Schwing he started a business in New York City. Folmer & Schwing first made gas lamps, then bicycles, and then photographic equipment, until cameras were all it made. Its first cameras appeared in its catalogue in 1896, and in 1898 Folmer designed the first Graflex, a large single-lens reflex camera. A mirror inside showed the photographer the picture, right way up, on a ground glass under a tall hood, so they could focus up to the moment of exposure.

The Graflex also had a [[g:focal-plane-shutter|focal-plane shutter]], a slit in a blind that ran across just in front of the plate. The first version was complicated and gave trouble, but in 1904 it was replaced by a cloth curtain shutter that was simple, reliable and fast. A camera that could focus right up to the moment of exposure and stop fast movement was what press and sports photographers wanted.

## Growth and turning points

The success of the focal-plane cameras caught George Eastman's eye. In 1905 [[m:kodak|Kodak]] bought the company, which became a Kodak division in Rochester, New York. In 1912 it brought out the [[speed-graphic|Speed Graphic]]. Graflex reflexes had been so popular with newspaper photographers that the company designed a camera just for them: a folding camera, lighter than the reflex, with the Graflex focal-plane shutter at the back and a second, leaf shutter in the lens. A photographer could use whichever suited the job.

By the 1920s Kodak had grown so big that the American government made it sell some of its businesses, including the Graflex division. No buyer came forward, and in 1926 the division was spun off as its own company, the Folmer Graflex Corporation. It redesigned its press cameras for the new [[flashbulb|flashbulbs]], and in 1933 it brought out the National Graflex, its first attempt at a camera for the mass market. In 1940 its Anniversary Graphics were updated to work better with flash and synchronized shutters, and in 1945 the company was renamed Graflex, Inc.

## What it made

The Speed Graphic became the American press camera. It took 4×5 inch sheet film, which printed sharply even on rough newsprint, and it was tough. Later models had a coupled rangefinder for quick focusing, a wire-frame finder for aiming, and a flash gun on the side. It was the still camera of the Second World War, and Joe Rosenthal used one for his photograph of the flag raising on Iwo Jima in 1945. In New York the press photographer Weegee used nothing else, partly, he said, because police let a photographer with a Speed Graphic through their lines. More Pulitzer Prizes were won with Graflex cameras than with any other maker's.

In 1947 the Pacemaker Speed Graphic simplified the focal-plane shutter to six speeds, and the Pacemaker Crown Graphic left it out altogether, which made the camera thinner and lighter. The Graflok back of 1949 let photographers fit roll-film backs to the big cameras. Graflex also made large studio and view cameras, aerial cameras for the armed forces, and, in the 1950s, small cameras: it bought the designs of the Ciro 35 and the Ciro-flex twin-lens reflex and sold them as the Graphic 35 and the Graflex 22. From the 1930s to 1957 the company grew from about 100 to 760 employees.

## Legacy

In 1958 Graflex was bought by General Precision, and in 1968 by the Singer Corporation. In 1965 it brought out the Graflex XL, a medium-format camera on the same idea as the [[m:mamiya|Mamiya]] Press. But Japanese cameras were taking over the consumer market, and professionals were moving to 35mm and medium-format SLRs such as the [[nikon-f|Nikon F]]. A photographer with a Speed Graphic had to change the film holder after every shot, while a 35mm camera held 36. Graflex was dissolved in 1973.

Its designs outlived it. In 1976 Cambo bought the tooling of the Graflex XL, and the Japanese view-camera maker Toyo bought the rights to the Speed and Crown Graphic. The old cameras are still used today by large-format photographers, who prize their big negatives, and for half a century of American news photography they were simply what a press camera looked like.
