## How it began

The screw-mount Leicas that followed the [[leica-i|Leica I]] had a separate rangefinder and viewfinder, and lenses screwed in slowly. In 1954 Leitz showed the M3, a new design with a bayonet mount, the M.

## How it works

The rangefinder image sits in the middle of a large, bright viewfinder. When a lens is mounted, the camera shows a matching frame line, which also corrects for parallax as you focus. Lenses lock in with a quarter turn.

## Why it mattered

Its finder was the brightest and most accurate of its time. The M3 sold more than 220,000 units and was used for some of the best-known reportage of the 1950s and 60s. It reportedly persuaded some Japanese makers to turn from rangefinders to SLRs, where they could compete.

## Limits and what replaced it

Rangefinders cannot show exactly what long or close-up lenses see. The SLR, led by the [[nikon-f|Nikon F]], took over professional work in the 1960s, but the M line continues.
