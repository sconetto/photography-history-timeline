## How it began

At the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, the engineer Harold Edgerton was studying electric motors in 1931. To see them at speed, he built a stroboscope: a lamp that flashed in step with the motor. He soon turned it on bullets, splashing milk and hummingbirds.

## How it works

A capacitor stores electricity at high voltage. A trigger makes the gas in a xenon tube conduct, and the stored charge rushes through it in a brilliant, very short flash. The capacitor then recharges for the next one.

## Why it mattered

Its flashes were short enough to freeze a bullet. Unlike [[flashbulb|flashbulbs]], it could fire thousands of times. Studio photographers adopted it, and small units made it the standard flash from the 1960s.

## Limits and what replaced it

Early units were heavy, with large batteries. Smaller electronics and automatic exposure control, and later through-the-lens flash metering, made it easy to use.
