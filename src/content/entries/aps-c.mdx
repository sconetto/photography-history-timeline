## How it began

Large sensors were very expensive to make, because chips are cut from wafers and bigger chips mean fewer per wafer and more defects. The first affordable professional digital SLRs used sensors about the size of a [[aps-film|APS film]] C frame. Nikon's D1 (1999) and Canon's EOS D30 (2000) set the pattern, and the name APS-C stuck.

## How it works

An APS-C sensor's diagonal is about 28mm, versus 43.3mm for 35mm film, so it records only the central part of the image a lens projects. The ratio of the diagonals is the [[g:crop-factor|crop factor]]: about 1.5, so a 50mm lens frames like a 75mm lens on full frame. Depth of field and total light gathered change too. See [[p:formats|formats and crop factor]].

## Why it mattered

APS-C made digital SLRs affordable to amateurs in the 2000s, and smaller lenses designed for the format kept systems light. It remains the most common sensor size in interchangeable-lens cameras.

## Limits and what replaced it

With less area it collects less light in total than [[full-frame-digital|full frame]], so it is noisier at the same exposure and gives less background blur at the same framing. Many professionals moved to full frame as its prices fell.
