## How it began

Camera obscuras had been rooms or tents. The German monk Johann Zahn published designs in 1685 for boxes small enough to carry, with a lens at the front, a mirror inside and a screen on top.

## How it works

The lens forms an image on the mirror, set at 45°, which reflects it up onto a sheet of ground glass or oiled paper. An artist could look down at the screen, right way up, and trace over it. Zahn also described ways to focus, and lenses of different strengths.

## Why it mattered

It was the camera in all but the plate. Box camera obscuras of this kind were used by artists through the eighteenth century, and Niépce and Daguerre adapted similar boxes. The mirror-and-screen layout returned in the reflex cameras of the twentieth century, such as the [[kine-exakta|Kine Exakta]] and the [[nikon-f|Nikon F]].

## Limits and what replaced it

It could only show an image, not keep one. When photography came, cameras briefly dropped the mirror for simplicity, until faster film made reflex viewing worthwhile again.
