## How it began

NASA wanted smaller, lighter, lower-power cameras for spacecraft. At the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, Eric Fossum and his team showed in 1993 that an image sensor could be made with the same CMOS processes used for computer chips, if each pixel had its own amplifier and a way to transfer its charge.

## How it works

As in a [[ccd|CCD]], light frees electrons in each [[g:pixel|pixel]]. But instead of shifting charge across the chip, each pixel converts its own charge to a voltage and is read out row by row, with timing, digitising and processing circuits on the same chip. That uses far less power and allows very fast readout. See [[p:silver|how light becomes an image]].

## Why it mattered

CMOS made image sensors cheap enough to put in phones, laptops and cars, and fast enough for high-speed bursts and video. Canon's use of its own CMOS sensors helped launch affordable [[full-frame-digital|full-frame]] and [[aps-c|APS-C]] digital SLRs. Back-illuminated (2009) and stacked designs later improved low-light quality and speed.

## Limits and what replaced it

Early CMOS sensors were noisier than CCDs, and reading rows one after another causes "rolling shutter" distortion with fast motion. Stacked sensors and, from 2023, global-shutter CMOS sensors in some cameras reduce this.
