## How it began

[[albumen-print|Albumen prints]] were made by laying a negative on paper in sunlight for many minutes. Leo Baekeland, a Belgian chemist in New York, made a paper with a silver chloride emulsion that needed only a short exposure to artificial light. It went on sale in 1893 as Velox.

## How it works

The paper is exposed briefly under a lamp in contact with the negative, which makes an invisible latent image. A [[g:developer|developer]] then brings out the picture, which is fixed and washed. Because development amplifies the image, much less light is needed.

## Why it mattered

Printing no longer depended on the sun and the weather. Kodak bought the rights in 1899, and the paper underpinned its developing and printing service for amateurs.

## Limits and what replaced it

It suited contact printing rather than enlarging. Faster bromide papers for the [[g:enlarger|enlarger]], and later colour papers, replaced it.
