## How it began

Eastman's first roll film used paper as a temporary support: the emulsion had to be stripped off the paper after development, a fiddly job done at the factory. In 1889 his chemist Henry Reichenbach patented a clear, flexible base of nitrocellulose. A clergyman, Hannibal Goodwin, had applied for a similar patent in 1887, and his heirs later won a large infringement case.

## How it works

The film base is a thin transparent sheet. A gelatin silver halide [[g:emulsion|emulsion]], like that of the [[gelatin-dry-plate|dry plate]], is coated on one side. Because it bends, many exposures fit on one roll that winds through the camera, and after development the negatives can be printed directly.

## Why it mattered

Flexible film made the hand camera, and photography for everyone, practical. Cut into narrow strips and perforated, it also made motion pictures possible, and [[cine-35mm|35mm cine film]] later became the format of the Leica.

## Limits and what replaced it

Nitrocellulose is highly flammable and decomposes with age; many early films and negatives have been lost to fire and decay. Cellulose acetate "safety film" took over by the early 1950s, and polyester bases followed.
