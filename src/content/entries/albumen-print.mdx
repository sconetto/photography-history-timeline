## How it began

Talbot's salted paper prints were soft, because the image sank into the paper fibres. In 1850 Louis Désiré Blanquart-Evrard coated paper with egg white first, so the image sat in a smooth layer on top.

## How it works

The albumen layer holds silver chloride. Pressed against a [[g:negative|negative]] in sunlight, the paper darkens where light passes, with no developer needed. The print is then toned with gold and fixed with [[g:fixer|fixer]].

## Why it mattered

Paired with sharp [[wet-collodion]] negatives, it gave fine detail cheaply and in quantity. Most nineteenth-century photographs that survive are albumen prints, including the carte de visite.

## Limits and what replaced it

Albumen prints yellow and crack with age. Gelatin silver papers, which could be developed and printed by artificial light, replaced them by the 1890s.
