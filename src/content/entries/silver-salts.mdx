## How it began

Around 1717 the German professor Johann Heinrich Schulze was trying to make a glowing substance. He mixed chalk with nitric acid that happened to contain silver. The mixture turned dark purple, but only on the side of the flask facing the window. When he heated a flask in the dark, nothing happened, so the cause was light, not heat.

## How it works

Silver salts such as silver nitrate and silver chloride are unstable in light. Light frees metallic silver, which looks dark because it is finely divided. Schulze stuck paper letters to the flask and found the words written in dark silver when he peeled them off. He had made the first image by light, but it lasted only until the rest darkened too.

## Why it mattered

This was the chemical half of photography. In 1777 Carl Wilhelm Scheele showed that violet light acts on silver chloride more strongly than red, and that the darkened part is metallic silver. Every silver-based process that followed, from the [[daguerreotype]] to colour film, builds on this.

## Limits and what replaced it

The images could not be kept: light went on darkening the unexposed silver. Making them permanent waited for a [[hypo-fixer|fixer]] that removes the unexposed salts, and for [[g:latent-image|development]], which made photographs fast enough to use.
