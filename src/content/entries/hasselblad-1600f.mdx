## How it began

Victor Hasselblad’s company built aerial cameras for the Swedish air force during the Second World War. After it, he designed a civilian camera, the 1600F, shown in 1948.

## How it works

The camera is a cube. A mirror reflects the image to a waist-level finder; lenses, finders and, crucially, film magazines can be swapped. A photographer could change from colour to black-and-white film mid-roll by swapping backs. From the 500C of 1957, [[g:leaf-shutter|leaf shutters]] in each lens allowed flash at any speed.

## Why it mattered

The modular design made it the camera of fashion, portrait and commercial studios for decades. NASA chose Hasselblads for spaceflight, and the photographs of the Apollo Moon landings were made on them.

## Limits and what replaced it

The 1600F’s focal-plane shutter was unreliable, which led to the leaf-shutter models. Digital backs extended the system’s life, but it was finally replaced by digital medium-format cameras.
