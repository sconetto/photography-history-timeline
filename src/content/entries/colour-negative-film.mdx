## How it began

Early colour films were slides, to be projected. In 1942 Kodak launched Kodacolor, a negative film from which colour prints could be made on paper. In 1972 Kodacolor II brought the C-41 process, which every maker adopted.

## How it works

The film has dye couplers in three layers, as in [[agfacolor-neu|Agfacolor]], but processing stops at a negative: colours and tones reversed. An orange mask built into the film corrects for impurities in the dyes. Printing on colour paper reverses it again into a positive.

## Why it mattered

Negatives were forgiving of exposure mistakes, and prints suited albums and wallets. Cheap processing in minilabs made colour the normal way to photograph from the 1970s, and colour negative film sold by the billion.

## Limits and what replaced it

Prints from negatives depend on the lab’s colour correction, and the film cannot be viewed directly. Digital cameras replaced it in the 2000s, but it remains the most popular film today.
