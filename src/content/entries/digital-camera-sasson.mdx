## How it began

In 1975 Steven Sasson, a young engineer at Kodak, was asked to see whether the new [[ccd|CCD]] chip could be used to capture images. He built a camera from a movie-camera lens, a Fairchild CCD, digital circuits and a portable cassette recorder.

## How it works

The CCD captured a 100 × 100 pixel black-and-white image. Circuits converted each pixel's charge into a number and wrote the numbers onto ordinary cassette tape, which took 23 seconds. A separate playback unit read the tape and showed the picture on a television set.

## Why it mattered

It was the first camera that captured, digitised and stored an image entirely electronically, without film. Kodak patented it but did not turn it into a product: its business was film. Two decades later the digital cameras its prototype foretold would overtake film, and Kodak's film business shrank sharply.

## Limits and what replaced it

At 0.01 megapixels and 3.6 kg it was a proof of concept. Megapixel sensors (Kodak, 1986), professional digital SLRs from 1991 and consumer cameras in the mid-1990s made the idea practical.
