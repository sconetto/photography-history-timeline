## How it began

Every other colour method of the time used filters and three exposures. The physicist Gabriel Lippmann looked for a way to record the wavelength of light directly, and announced one in 1891.

## How it works

A glass plate with an extremely fine-grained [[g:emulsion|emulsion]] is exposed with a pool of mercury behind it. Light reflects off the mercury and interferes with itself, making a pattern of standing waves in the emulsion. After development, layers of silver are spaced by half a wavelength of each colour, and in reflected light they send back the original colour.

## Why it mattered

It was the first direct, single-exposure colour photograph, and earned Lippmann the 1908 Nobel Prize in Physics. Its colours could be startlingly accurate.

## Limits and what replaced it

Plates needed long exposures, could be seen only at one angle, and could not be copied. The [[autochrome]] of 1907 was far more practical, and Lippmann’s method faded from use.
