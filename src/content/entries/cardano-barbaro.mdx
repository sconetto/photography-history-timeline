## How it began

For centuries the [[camera-obscura]] used a bare hole, which gives a dim, soft image. In 1550 Gerolamo Cardano wrote of putting a glass disc in the opening. In 1568 the Venetian Daniele Barbaro described using an old man’s spectacle lens, and found that covering part of it so that only a small opening remained made the image sharper.

## How it works

A lens gathers light from a much larger opening than a pinhole and brings it to a focus, so the image is far brighter. But a simple lens is sharp only near its centre. Making the opening smaller, which we now call stopping down or closing the [[g:aperture|aperture]], cuts out the edge rays that blur the image, at the cost of light.

## Why it mattered

Barbaro’s trade-off between brightness and sharpness is the basic choice every photographer still makes. With a lens, the camera obscura became a practical drawing aid for artists, and the device Niépce and Daguerre would later fit with light-sensitive plates. See [[p:pinhole|light and the pinhole]].

## Limits and what replaced it

Single spectacle lenses had strong [[g:aberration|aberrations]] and fringes of colour. Better lenses, designed by calculation from the nineteenth century, replaced them.
