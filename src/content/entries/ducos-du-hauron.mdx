## How it began

[[maxwell-colour|Maxwell]] had shown in 1861 that three images, through red, green and blue filters, could recreate a colour scene by projecting coloured light. In 1868 the French inventor Louis Ducos du Hauron patented a set of methods for making colour pictures on paper, and published them in 1869. The poet and inventor Charles Cros reached the same idea at the same time.

## How it works

Adding light starts from black; printing starts from white paper. So Ducos du Hauron printed each separation in the complementary colour: cyan absorbs red, magenta absorbs green, yellow absorbs blue. Stacked, the three layers subtract light from white to rebuild the colours.

## Why it mattered

This subtractive scheme became the basis of colour printing, of [[kodachrome|Kodachrome]] and every colour film, and of the cyan, magenta and yellow inks in printers today. He also described the additive screen method used later by the [[autochrome]].

## Limits and what replaced it

The plates of the 1860s could barely record green and red, so his pictures needed very long exposures and were rare. Colour-sensitised emulsions made the methods practical decades later.
