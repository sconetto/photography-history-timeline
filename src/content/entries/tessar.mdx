## How it began

Paul Rudolph, a mathematician turned designer at Zeiss, had already made the Protar and the Unar. In 1902 he combined ideas from both into a four-element lens and named it Tessar, from the Greek for four.

## How it works

Like the [[cooke-triplet]] it has two convex lenses around a concave one, but the rear convex lens is a cemented pair of different glasses. That extra freedom corrects colour and edge sharpness better, without adding air spaces that would scatter light.

## Why it mattered

The Tessar gave high sharpness at moderate cost and size, and Zeiss licensed it widely. It became the standard lens on good folding cameras, twin-lens reflexes and early 35mm cameras, and was imitated under dozens of names once the patent ran out.

## Limits and what replaced it

Its aperture stayed modest, around f/2.8 at best. Faster lenses, such as Double Gauss designs like the Planar, took over for low light, and computer-designed lenses for wide angles and zooms.
