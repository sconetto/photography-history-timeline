## How it began

At Bell Labs in New Jersey, Willard Boyle and George Smith were asked to come up with a new kind of semiconductor memory. On 17 October 1969, in a short brainstorming session, they sketched a device that stores charge in a row of tiny capacitors and moves it along step by step. They soon saw it could also capture images.

## How it works

When light strikes silicon, it frees electrons: the photoelectric effect. Each [[g:pixel|pixel]] of a CCD collects these electrons in a potential well, so the charge is proportional to the light. To read the image, voltages on the electrodes shift every charge packet to the next well, row by row, like a bucket brigade, until each reaches a single amplifier at the edge and is measured. See [[p:silver|how light becomes an image]].

## Why it mattered

The CCD was the first practical electronic image sensor. It went into the [[digital-camera-sasson|first digital camera]] (1975), video cameras, scanners, space telescopes and the first generation of digital still cameras. Boyle and Smith shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

## Limits and what replaced it

CCDs need special manufacturing, use a lot of power and read out slowly through one amplifier. [[cmos-sensor|CMOS active-pixel sensors]], with an amplifier in every pixel, overtook them in cameras and phones in the 2000s.
