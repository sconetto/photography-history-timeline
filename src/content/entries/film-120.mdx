## How it began

Kodak introduced 120 film in 1901 for its Brownie No. 2 box camera. It was one of many roll-film sizes of the time, each tied to particular cameras, and most of the others died out.

## How it works

The film is rolled on a spool together with a longer strip of opaque paper. Frame numbers are printed on the back of the paper, and on simple cameras the photographer winds until the next number appears in a small red window. Different cameras divide the roll into frames of 6×4.5, 6×6, 6×7 or 6×9 cm.

## Why it mattered

Its negatives are three to four times the area of [[format-135|35mm]], so they enlarge with finer [[g:grain|grain]]. After the [[rolleiflex|Rolleiflex]] and the [[hasselblad-1600f|Hasselblad]], 120 became the professional medium format for portraits, fashion and landscape.

## Limits and what replaced it

A roll gives only 8 to 16 shots. Digital medium-format backs and cameras took over professional work, but 120 is still made and is popular again with film photographers.
