## How it began

In the 1890s the best lenses were complex and expensive. H. Dennis Taylor, an optician at T. Cooke & Sons in York, worked out in 1893 that a pair of convex lenses with a concave lens between them could do the same job with only three pieces of glass.

## How it works

The concave middle element cancels the curvature of field and astigmatism that the outer convex elements produce, while their spacing balances the other [[g:aberration|aberrations]]. The result is an anastigmat: a lens sharp across a flat picture, not just at the centre.

## Why it mattered

It was cheap to make and performed well, and it became a standard for amateur and box cameras. Its arrangement became a starting point for designers, who split and cemented its elements into new lenses, including the [[tessar]].

## Limits and what replaced it

Three elements leave little room to correct for wide angles or wide apertures. Four-, six- and many-element designs took over for demanding work.
