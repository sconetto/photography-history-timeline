## How it began

Early 35mm SLRs such as the [[kine-exakta|Kine Exakta]] were viewed from above, and the image was reversed left to right. In 1949 the Contax S, made by Zeiss Ikon in Dresden, put a [[g:pentaprism|pentaprism]] on top of the screen; the Italian Rectaflex had been shown with one shortly before.

## How it works

The mirror throws the image up onto the focusing screen. A five-sided prism with a roof edge reflects it several times inside the glass, turning it right way round, and passes it out through the eyepiece at eye level.

## Why it mattered

Photographers could hold an SLR to the eye, follow moving subjects naturally, and see exactly what the lens saw. Combined with the [[instant-return-mirror|instant-return mirror]], this made the 35mm SLR the dominant camera from the 1960s.

## Limits and what replaced it

Prisms are heavy and give a dim view at small apertures. Electronic viewfinders, which show the sensor’s image directly, replaced them in mirrorless cameras.
