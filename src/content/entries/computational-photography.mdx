## How it began

Phone sensors are tiny, so a single exposure in dim light is noisy. Researchers, including Marc Levoy's group at Google, applied ideas from computer graphics: take a burst of frames, align them and merge them. Google's HDR+ shipped in 2013, portrait modes in 2016 and Night Sight in 2018.

## How it works

Random noise falls with the square root of the number of frames averaged. Sixteen frames give a quarter of the noise of one. Short frames avoid blur; merging them recovers detail in shadows and highlights. Depth from two cameras or from machine learning lets software blur the background. Learned models now help with demosaicing, sharpening, colour and zoom.

## Why it mattered

Software made small sensors produce images that once needed much bigger cameras, and changed what a photograph is: a computed estimate of a scene, built from many exposures. The same tools raise new questions about authenticity, now addressed by content-provenance standards such as C2PA.

## Limits and what replaced it

Heavy processing can look artificial, and merging can fail with fast-moving subjects. The line between correcting and inventing detail is blurred, especially with generative AI.
