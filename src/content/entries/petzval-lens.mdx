## How it began

Daguerre's cameras used a slow landscape lens, so a portrait meant sitting still in bright sun for minutes. In 1840 Joseph Petzval, a mathematics professor in Vienna, took up the problem. With a team of army gunners as human calculators, he computed a lens of four elements in two groups instead of finding one by trial and error. The Voigtländer company made and sold it from 1841.

## How it works

The amount of light a lens passes depends on its [[g:f-number|f-number]], the focal length divided by the width of the opening. Petzval's design worked at about f/3.6, roughly sixteen to twenty times brighter than the f/15 or so of the 1839 lens. It is sharp in the centre and softer at the edges, which suits a portrait. See [[p:pinhole|why a lens gathers more light than a pinhole]].

## Why it mattered

Together with faster chemistry it cut daguerreotype portraits to tens of seconds or less, which made the portrait studio a business. It was also one of the first lenses designed by calculation, a method that became the rule.

## Limits and what replaced it

It has a narrow field and a curved field of focus, so it is poor for landscapes or architecture. Anastigmats of the 1890s, such as the Cooke triplet and Zeiss Tessar, corrected these faults.
