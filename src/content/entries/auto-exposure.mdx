## How it began

After the [[light-meter|light meter]], photographers still had to set the camera by hand. Kodak’s Super Six-20 of 1938 linked a selenium meter to the [[g:aperture|aperture]], setting it automatically; it was expensive and few were sold.

## How it works

The meter measures the light. In shutter priority, the photographer picks a [[g:shutter-speed|shutter speed]] and the camera sets the aperture; in aperture priority it is the other way round; in program mode the camera picks both. Early systems used needles and mechanical linkages, and later ones electronics.

## Why it mattered

It removed the most common reason for a failed photograph. Cheap “electric eye” cameras of the 1960s, and the [[canon-ae1|Canon AE-1]], made automatic exposure normal.

## Limits and what replaced it

A meter is fooled by snow, backlight or dark scenes. [[ttl-metering|Through-the-lens]] and multi-zone metering, and today’s live view of the actual exposure, made it more reliable.
