## How it began

The idea of forming dyes during development dates from Rudolf Fischer in 1912, but the couplers would wander between layers. [[kodachrome|Kodachrome]] (1935) avoided this by adding the couplers in processing, which needed complex laboratory machines. In 1936 Agfa chemists found ways to anchor the couplers in each layer of the film.

## How it works

The film has three [[g:emulsion|emulsions]], sensitive to blue, green and red. As the [[g:developer|developer]] turns exposed silver into metal, its by-products react with the coupler in that layer to form a yellow, magenta or cyan dye. The silver is then bleached away, leaving a dye image.

## Why it mattered

Processing became simple enough for ordinary labs, and later for amateurs. After the Second World War Agfa’s patents were taken as reparations, and its approach became the basis of most colour films worldwide, including [[colour-negative-film|colour negative film]].

## Limits and what replaced it

Early dyes faded and colours were less saturated than Kodachrome. Improved couplers and the C-41 and E-6 processes refined the method rather than replacing it.
