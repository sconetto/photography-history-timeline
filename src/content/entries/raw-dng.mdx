## How it began

Cameras turn sensor data into a [[jpeg|JPEG]] using fixed settings. Professional cameras could save the unprocessed data, the [[g:raw|raw]] file, in formats specific to each maker. In 2004 Adobe published DNG, an open format for raw data.

## How it works

The raw file holds the values from every photosite under the [[bayer-filter|Bayer filter]], usually 12 or 14 bits each. Software does the [[g:demosaicing|demosaicing]], sets the [[g:white-balance|white balance]] and applies a tone curve, and the photographer can redo these at any time.

## Why it mattered

Raw editing became the digital darkroom: more room to correct exposure and colour, without losing quality. DNG was intended to keep files readable when makers dropped formats.

## Limits and what replaced it

Files are large and need processing. Most makers still use their own formats; phones mix raw data with [[computational-photography|computational processing]].
