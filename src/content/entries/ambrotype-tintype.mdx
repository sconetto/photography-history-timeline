## How it began

The [[wet-collodion]] process made sharp glass negatives in seconds. Photographers soon found that a thin, underexposed collodion negative looks like a positive when seen against black. James Ambrose Cutting patented a version on glass in 1854, and Hamilton Smith one on thin iron plates in 1856.

## How it works

The silver image in collodion looks pale grey by reflected light. Backed with black varnish, velvet or a black-enamelled metal plate, the clear shadows show dark and the silver highlights show light, so the negative reads as a positive.

## Why it mattered

They were much cheaper than daguerreotypes and needed no printing. Tintypes were tough enough to post or carry, and itinerant photographers made them at fairs and battlefields. For many families they were the first portraits they could afford.

## Limits and what replaced it

Each one was unique and could not be copied, and the process meant a portable darkroom. Gelatin dry plates and paper prints made them obsolete by around 1900, until wet-plate enthusiasts revived them in the 2000s.
