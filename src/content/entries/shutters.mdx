## How it began

Early plates needed seconds or minutes, and photographers simply removed and replaced the lens cap. The [[gelatin-dry-plate|gelatin dry plate]] made exposures of a hundredth of a second possible, and these needed a mechanism. In the 1880s Ottomar Anschütz used a slit in a blind running just in front of the plate. In 1912 the Compur leaf shutter from Friedrich Deckel set a standard for lens shutters.

## How it works

A [[g:focal-plane-shutter|focal-plane shutter]] uses two curtains: the first opens and the second follows, so a slit sweeps across the film. A [[g:leaf-shutter|leaf shutter]] uses blades that open and close in the lens. Each makes a series of [[g:shutter-speed|shutter speeds]], each half the one before.

## Why it mattered

Shutters made action and handheld photography possible. Focal-plane shutters let lenses be changed easily and reached very short speeds; leaf shutters were quiet and worked with flash at any speed.

## Limits and what replaced it

A focal-plane slit can distort fast subjects and limits flash speed; leaf shutters top out around 1/500 s. Electronic shutters avoid moving parts but bring their own distortions, which [[stacked-sensor|stacked sensors]] reduce.
