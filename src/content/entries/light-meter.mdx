## How it began

Before meters, photographers judged [[g:exposure|exposure]] from experience, printed tables or extinction meters that relied on the eye. In 1932 the Weston company sold a meter built around a selenium cell, which makes a current when light falls on it.

## How it works

The cell's current grows with the brightness of the scene, and a needle shows it. A dial then converts that reading into pairs of [[g:aperture|aperture]] and shutter speed for the speed of the film loaded.

## Why it mattered

It made exposure a measurement. It also led directly to meters built into cameras and then to automatic exposure.

## Limits and what replaced it

Selenium cells were bulky and weak in dim light; CdS and silicon cells followed, and from 1963 meters moved behind the lens. Today the camera's own sensor does the job.
